<KeyTakeaways>
- Accept fast, deliver later: the API's only job is to durably record the request and return `202 Accepted`; everything slow happens behind a queue.
- Delivery is at-least-once all the way down, so every layer deduplicates, from the API's idempotency key to a send log to the provider's collapse id.
- Separate lanes by priority, because a one-time password must never wait behind a 50-million-user marketing campaign.
- The hardest part is not sending; it is deciding not to send: preferences, quiet hours, frequency caps, and digests protect the person on the other end.
- Third-party providers set your real limits. Their quotas, token lifecycles, and outages shape the design more than your own servers do.
</KeyTakeaways>

Every product eventually needs to tap someone on the shoulder. Your order shipped. Someone replied to your comment. Here is your login code. It looks like the easiest feature in the backlog: call Apple, call Google, call an email provider, done.

Then the product grows. A marketing campaign goes out to 40 million people and password-reset emails stop arriving for an hour. A retry loop sends the same "Your payment failed" push four times. A user who turned off promotional email gets one anyway, and complains to their email provider, and your domain's reputation drops for everyone. The push provider starts rejecting your requests because you exceeded a quota you did not know existed.

A notification service is where reliability engineering meets human attention. In this chapter we design one that sends a billion notifications a day across push, email, SMS, and an in-app inbox, and we will spend as much time on the notifications we choose *not* to send as on the ones we do.

## Problem and scope

We are building an internal platform. Other teams' services, which we call **producers**, ask it to notify a user about something. The platform decides whether, when, and how to deliver that notification, hands it to the right third-party provider, and tracks what happened.

**In scope:**
- Channels: iOS push through Apple Push Notification service (APNs), Android push through Firebase Cloud Messaging (FCM), email, SMS, and an in-app inbox.
- Two traffic shapes: **transactional** notifications for one user ("your code is 482913") and **bulk** campaigns to a segment of users ("20% off this weekend").
- User preferences per category and channel, quiet hours, and time zones.
- Templates with localization and personalization.
- Scheduling, including "deliver at 9:00 in each user's local time".
- Frequency caps, deduplication, and delivery tracking.

**Out of scope:**
- Building email or SMS transport. We integrate with providers; we do not run mail servers or connect to carriers.
- The persistent-connection gateway that streams live updates into open apps. That is its own system; we only write to the inbox it reads.
- Machine-learned relevance ranking. We leave hooks for it in [Evolution](#evolution).
- The UI that marketers use to author campaigns.

<Callout type="interview" title="Scope it in the first five minutes">
"Design a notification system" is deliberately vague. Before drawing anything, pin down four things out loud: which channels, transactional versus bulk (or both), the scale in notifications per day, and whether the system decides *what* to send or only *how* to deliver it. Interviewers listen for whether you separate the platform from the products that use it.
</Callout>

## Requirements

### Functional

1. A producer can send a notification to one user, either by calling an API or by publishing an event.
2. A producer can launch a campaign to a segment of up to 50M users, immediately or on a schedule.
3. Users control which categories they receive on which channels, set quiet hours, and have a time zone.
4. Notifications are rendered from versioned, localized templates with per-user data.
5. Producers can query the delivery status of any notification they sent.
6. The platform enforces per-user frequency caps and never shows the same notification twice.

### Non-functional

- **Throughput:** 1B notifications per day, with peaks around 58k notifications per second (derived below).
- **Latency:** for high-priority notifications (login codes, security alerts), <Term id="p99">p99</Term> under 5 s from API acceptance to provider handoff. Bulk notifications are best-effort and may take minutes.
- **Availability:** the ingestion API is 99.99% available (≈ 53 min of downtime a year). Delivery may lag during incidents, but accepting requests must not stop.
- **Durability:** once we return `202 Accepted`, the notification is never silently lost. It is delivered, suppressed by a rule, expired, or dead-lettered, and every outcome is recorded.
- **Delivery semantics:** <Term id="at-least-once-delivery">at-least-once</Term> internally, with deduplication so that users almost never see duplicates.

Notice what is *not* on the list: exactly-once delivery and global ordering. We will see why both are expensive and why users do not need them.

## Back-of-the-envelope estimation

**Assumptions:**
- 200M daily active users (DAU), each receiving 5 notifications per day on average.
- Peak traffic is 5× the average (morning commutes, campaign launches, live events).
- Channel mix: push 60%, in-app 25%, email 13%, SMS 2%.
- Half of push traffic goes to Android devices.
- A notification record is about 500 B; each one produces about 4 status events of 100 B.
- 500M registered users with 2 devices each on average.
- A provider call takes about 100 ms end to end.

<Estimate title="Traffic">
| Quantity                 | Formula                    | Result            |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------- | ----------------- |
| Notifications per day    | 200M DAU × 5               | 1B / day          |
| Average rate             | 1B ÷ 86,400 s              | ≈ 11.6k msg/s     |
| Peak rate                | 11.6k × 5                  | ≈ 58k msg/s       |
| Push at peak             | 58k × 60%                  | ≈ 34.7k msg/s     |
| Android push at peak     | 34.7k × 50% × 60 s         | ≈ 1.04M msg/min   |
| Email per day            | 1B × 13%                   | 130M / day        |
| SMS per day              | 1B × 2%                    | 20M / day         |
| SMS at peak              | 20M ÷ 86,400 s × 5         | ≈ 1.2k msg/s      |
</Estimate>

Two of those numbers should make you stop.

First, **1.04M Android messages per minute**. FCM's HTTP v1 API enforces a per-project quota on downstream messages, and the default is 600k messages per minute <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/throttling-and-quotas" title="FCM throttling and quotas" author="Firebase" />. Our peak is nearly double that. Increases are granted in steps, and only when existing usage is consistently close to the current limit, so this is a capacity-planning conversation that starts months before launch, not on launch day.

Second, **1.2k SMS per second**. SMS throughput is allocated per sender. Twilio's documented defaults are 3 message segments per second for a US toll-free number and 100 per second for a short code <Cite href="https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/115002943027-Understanding-Twilio-Rate-Limits-and-Message-Queues" title="Understanding Twilio rate limits and message queues" author="Twilio" />. Our peak is about 12 short codes' worth of default throughput. SMS is also by far the most expensive channel per message, which is why it is only 2% of our traffic and why we route as much as we can to push.

<Estimate title="Storage">
| Quantity                       | Formula                         | Result           |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| Notification records per day   | 1B × 500 B                      | 500 GB / day     |
| Hot retention (90 days)        | 500 GB × 90                     | 45 TB            |
| With 3× replication            | 45 TB × 3                       | 135 TB           |
| Status events per day          | 1B × 4 × 100 B                  | 400 GB / day     |
| Device registry                | 500M × 2 devices × 200 B        | 200 GB           |
| Preferences                    | 500M × 1 KB                     | 500 GB           |
| In-app inbox (30 days)         | 1B × 25% × 30 × 500 B           | 3.75 TB          |
</Estimate>

Storage is large but unremarkable. The device registry and preferences fit comfortably in memory across a cache cluster, which matters because we read them for every notification.

<Estimate title="Concurrency">
| Quantity                              | Formula                     | Result              |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------- |
| In-flight provider calls at peak      | 58k msg/s × 0.1 s           | ≈ 5.8k              |
| …if providers slow to 1 s             | 58k msg/s × 1 s             | ≈ 58k               |
| Status writes at peak                 | 58k × 4 events              | ≈ 232k writes/s     |
| 50M-user campaign at 40k msg/s        | 50M ÷ 40k                   | ≈ 21 min            |
</Estimate>

The first two rows come from Little's law: the number of requests in flight equals the arrival rate times the time each request takes. A provider that slows from 100 ms to 1 s multiplies our required concurrency by ten. A slow provider is often more dangerous than a dead one, because a dead one fails fast. We return to this in [Living with providers](#living-with-providers).

The third row is easy to miss: status updates, not notifications, are our heaviest write path.

## API design

Producers talk to us in two ways. Synchronous HTTP suits request-driven flows such as login codes. Asynchronous events suit flows that already publish domain events ("order shipped"). Both paths land in the same request topic, so everything downstream is identical.

### Send one notification

```http title="Request"
POST /v1/notifications
Authorization: Bearer <service token>
Idempotency-Key: order-8812-shipped
Content-Type: application/json

{
  "recipient": { "user_id": "u_51f2c9" },
  "category": "order_updates",
  "template": "order_shipped",
  "data": { "order_id": "8812", "eta": "2026-10-27" },
  "priority": "normal",
  "channels": "auto",
  "ttl_seconds": 86400,
  "collapse_key": "order-8812"
}
```

```http title="Response"
HTTP/1.1 202 Accepted
Content-Type: application/json

{ "notification_id": "01JB7Q4ZK3N0X8Y5V2M6T9RWHF", "status": "accepted" }
```

A few deliberate choices here:

- **`202 Accepted`, not `200 OK`.** We have recorded the request, not delivered it. Pretending otherwise invites producers to build on a guarantee we cannot make.
- **`Idempotency-Key` header.** If the producer times out and retries, we return the original `notification_id` instead of creating a second notification. This follows the IETF draft for an `Idempotency-Key` header <Cite href="https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-httpapi-idempotency-key-header/" title="The Idempotency-Key HTTP Header Field" author="IETF HTTPAPI Working Group" />; see [Idempotency](/chapters/idempotency) for the general technique.
- **`category`, not channel.** Producers say *what kind* of notification this is. Users' preferences are expressed per category, and the platform picks channels. A producer can request specific channels, but `auto` is the default.
- **`ttl_seconds`.** A "your driver is arriving" push is worthless ten minutes later. Every notification carries an expiry, and stale ones are dropped rather than delivered late.
- **`collapse_key`.** Later notifications with the same key replace earlier ones on the device, so "order shipped" can be replaced by "out for delivery" instead of stacking.

### The rest of the surface

| Endpoint                                   | Purpose                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `POST /v1/campaigns`                       | Send a template to a segment, now or at a scheduled local time |
| `GET /v1/notifications/{id}`               | Per-channel status: queued, sent, delivered, failed, suppressed |
| `PUT /v1/users/{id}/preferences`           | Opt in or out per category × channel; quiet hours; time zone   |
| `POST /v1/users/{id}/devices`              | Register or refresh a <Term id="device-token">device token</Term> |
| `DELETE /v1/users/{id}/devices/{device_id}`| Unregister on logout                                           |
| `POST /internal/providers/{name}/events`   | Webhooks from providers: delivered, bounced, complained        |

Campaigns return a `campaign_id` and are always asynchronous. Status queries are eventually consistent: a notification may show `queued` for a moment after the push has already arrived. That is acceptable for a status page and keeps the write path cheap.

## Data model

Our access patterns drive the storage choices:

1. Write a notification record — 1B times a day, rarely updated afterwards.
2. Read a user's recent notifications for the inbox, newest first.
3. Look up a notification by id for status queries.
4. Read a user's devices and preferences — on every single notification.
5. Find a device by its token when a provider says the token is dead.

<FigRef id="data-model" /> shows the core tables.

**Notifications** are write-heavy, append-mostly, time-ordered per user, and expire after 90 days. That is the textbook fit for a wide-column store such as Cassandra: partition by `user_id`, cluster by a time-ordered `notification_id` (a ULID or time-based UUID) in descending order, and set a row TTL so old data deletes itself. Each user's partition stays small: 5 notifications a day for 90 days is 450 rows, about 225 KB. A second table keyed by `notification_id` alone serves status lookups.

**Deliveries** record one row per notification per channel: its state, attempt count, and the provider's message id. This is where the 232k writes per second land, so we write only state *transitions* and batch them.

**Devices** are keyed by `user_id`, with a secondary index on `token`. When APNs reports a token as unregistered, we need to find and delete it without knowing the user.

**Preferences** are small, read on every notification, and rarely written. They live in a durable store with a cache in front; a 95% cache hit rate turns 58k reads per second into about 2.9k database reads.

**Templates** are versioned and localized, and they are few and edited by humans, so a relational database is the right home. Workers cache compiled templates in memory.

## High-level design

Start with the simplest thing that could work, shown in <FigRef id="naive-design" />: the order service calls each provider directly when an order ships.

<Figure
  id="naive-design"
  src="./figures/naive-design.svg"
  alt="An order service makes synchronous HTTPS calls directly to APNs, FCM, an email provider, and an SMS provider; the SMS call is highlighted as failing."
  caption="The naive design. Every producer integrates with every provider, synchronously."
/>

It works for a demo and fails in production in at least five ways:

1. **Latency leaks into the product.** The checkout request now waits on four external calls. When the SMS provider takes 3 s, checkout takes 3 s.
2. **Failures leak too.** If the SMS call fails, does the order fail? Retrying inline blocks the request; not retrying loses the message.
3. **Every team reimplements everything.** Device tokens, preferences, templates, quiet hours, retries, and provider credentials end up copied into every service, each copy slightly different.
4. **Nobody sees the whole picture.** Ten teams each send "reasonable" volumes, and together they send a user 40 notifications a day. LinkedIn built its Air Traffic Controller as a single decision point for every member notification after members told them they were getting too many <Cite href="https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/messaging-notifications/air-traffic-controller-member-first-notifications-at-linkedin" title="Air Traffic Controller: Member-first notifications at LinkedIn" author="Changji Shi, Adriel Fuad" year="2018" />.
5. **Provider limits are shared but enforced nowhere.** The FCM quota is per project, not per team.

A tempting middle ground is a shared client library: every team imports one well-written `notify()` function. It fixes the duplicated code in point 3 and nothing else. The calls are still synchronous inside each producer, the frequency caps still cannot see across teams, and the provider quota is still consumed by whoever happens to be busiest. Problems 1, 2, 4, and 5 are all about *shared state and shared capacity*, and a library cannot own either. A service can.

The fix is a dedicated platform with a queue at its front door. <FigRef id="high-level-design" /> shows the design we will refine for the rest of the chapter.

<Figure
  id="high-level-design"
  src="./figures/high-level-design.svg"
  alt="Producer services call the Notification API, which checks an idempotency store and appends to a request topic. A notification processor consumes the topic, looks up templates, preferences, and the device registry, and writes one message per channel to push, email, SMS, and in-app topics. Channel workers send to providers or write to the inbox store. Providers send delivery webhooks to a tracking service, which updates a status store."
  caption="High-level design. Numbers match the walkthrough below; the highlighted edge is the provider boundary we do not control."
  width="wide"
/>

<Steps>
1. A producer calls `POST /v1/notifications` (or publishes an event that an adapter turns into the same request).
2. The Notification API authenticates the producer, validates the payload, and checks the idempotency key. A repeat key returns the original `notification_id`.
3. The API appends the request to a durable, replicated request topic and only then returns `202 Accepted`.
4. The notification processor consumes the topic. The topic is partitioned by `user_id`, so all of one user's notifications go to the same processor instance.
5. The processor loads preferences, devices, and the template, applies the rules in [Respecting the human](#respecting-the-human), renders the content, and decides which channels to use.
6. It publishes one message per chosen channel to that channel's topic.
7. Channel workers send each message to its provider (APNs, FCM, the email provider, the SMS provider) or write it to the in-app inbox store, recording `sent` or `failed`.
8. Providers report later outcomes (delivered, bounced, marked as spam) through webhooks; the tracking service records them in the status store.
</Steps>

Why this shape?

- **The API is thin.** It does three cheap things: authenticate, deduplicate, append. That is how we get 99.99% availability for ingestion even when a provider, the preference service, or a template is broken.
- **The request topic is a shock absorber.** A campaign launch or a provider outage turns into queue lag, not dropped requests. We use a log-based broker (Kafka or similar) because we want replay and per-partition ordering by user; see [Message queues](/chapters/message-queues).
- **One topic per channel** isolates channels from each other. An email provider outage backs up the email topic; push keeps flowing.
- **The processor is where policy lives.** Preferences, caps, quiet hours, and channel choice are decided once, in one place, instead of in every producer.

## Deep dives

### Never lose a notification, rarely send one twice [#reliable-delivery]

Our durability promise starts before our API and ends after the provider. Let us walk the chain and find every place a notification can be lost or duplicated.

**Producer to API.** Consider the order service. It commits "order shipped" to its database, then calls our API. If it crashes between the two, the notification is lost. If it calls first and the commit fails, we notify about something that never happened. This is the dual-write problem, and the standard fix is the <Term id="transactional-outbox">transactional outbox</Term>: the producer writes the notification request into an outbox table in the same transaction as the business change, and a relay publishes it afterwards <Cite href="https://microservices.io/patterns/data/transactional-outbox" title="Pattern: Transactional outbox" author="Chris Richardson" />. The relay may publish the same row twice after a crash, which is fine, because the request carries an idempotency key.

**API to topic.** The API returns `202` only after the request topic has acknowledged the write on multiple replicas (for Kafka, `acks=all` with a minimum in-sync replica count of 2). If the broker is unreachable, the API returns `503` and the producer retries with the same idempotency key. We never acknowledge a request we have not durably stored.

The idempotency store maps `(producer_id, idempotency_key)` to a `notification_id` for 24 hours. The insert is conditional ("insert if absent"), so two concurrent retries with the same key cannot both win.

**Topic to processor to channel topics.** Consumers commit their offset only after publishing downstream. A processor that crashes mid-batch replays from its last committed offset and republishes some messages. That is at-least-once, and the duplicates carry the same `notification_id`, so the next layer can recognize them.

<Figure
  id="notification-lifecycle"
  src="./figures/notification-lifecycle.svg"
  alt="A state machine. Accepted moves to processing. Processing moves to suppressed, scheduled, or queued. Scheduled moves to queued when its timer fires. Queued moves to sending. Sending moves to sent on success or retry_wait on timeout, 429, or 5xx. Retry_wait returns to queued after backoff, or moves to expired when the TTL passes, or to dead_lettered when attempts are exhausted. Sent moves to delivered when the channel reports a receipt."
  caption="Notification lifecycle per channel. Every path ends in a recorded terminal state; none ends in silence."
/>

<FigRef id="notification-lifecycle" /> shows the states one delivery moves through. The important property is that every path ends somewhere visible: `sent`, `delivered`, `suppressed`, `expired`, or `dead_lettered`. "We don't know what happened" is not a state.

**Worker to provider: the unknown outcome.** This is the genuinely hard step. A worker sends a push to APNs and the request times out. Did Apple receive it? We cannot know. If we retry, the user might get it twice. If we do not, they might not get it at all.

We handle it with three layers of defense, shown in <FigRef id="retry-and-dedup" />.

<Figure
  id="retry-and-dedup"
  src="./figures/retry-and-dedup.svg"
  alt="Sequence diagram. The push worker claims the notification in the send log, posts to APNs with a collapse id equal to the notification id, and times out. It requeues to the retry topic with a 2 second delay, is redelivered, sees the claim is still attempting, retries with the same collapse id, receives 200 OK, and marks it sent. A later duplicate redelivery finds the claim already sent and is skipped."
  caption="Retrying an unknown outcome safely. The send log stops duplicates we can see; the collapse id makes the ones we cannot see harmless on the device."
  width="wide"
/>

1. **A send log.** Before calling a provider, the worker claims `(notification_id, channel)` with a conditional write. The row moves from absent to `attempting` to `sent`. A redelivered message whose row says `sent` is skipped. This catches every duplicate caused by *our* retries and crashes.
2. **A provider-side collapse id.** For the duplicates we cannot see — the timed-out request that actually succeeded — we make the second copy harmless. APNs accepts an `apns-collapse-id` header, and notifications with the same collapse id replace each other on the device rather than stacking <Cite href="https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/sending-notification-requests-to-apns" title="Sending notification requests to APNs" author="Apple" />. On Android, a notification `tag` does the same in the notification drawer. We set both to the `notification_id`.
3. **A per-category policy for channels without a collapse mechanism.** Email and SMS have no equivalent: a duplicate email is a duplicate email. So each category declares what to do on an unknown outcome. A login code says `retry`; a second copy of a code is harmless, a missing one locks the user out. A marketing email says `do_not_retry`; one lost promotion is better than a duplicate that gets reported as spam.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Exactly-once is a trap here">
Exactly-once processing inside a broker does not extend to an HTTP call to Apple. Once a side effect leaves your system, the best you can do is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent or collapsible effects. Saying this clearly in an interview is worth more than any amount of distributed-transaction machinery.
</Callout>

**Retries without blocking.** A failed send must wait before retrying, but a worker that sleeps holds its partition hostage: every message behind it waits too. Instead, the worker publishes the message to a **delay topic** (`push-retry-2s`, `push-retry-30s`, `push-retry-5m`, …) and moves on. Each delay topic is consumed by workers that wait until the message's due time. Delays follow <Term id="exponential-backoff">exponential backoff</Term> with random jitter, so a provider recovering from an outage is not hit by a synchronized wall of retries <Cite href="https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/exponential-backoff-and-jitter/" title="Exponential Backoff And Jitter" author="Marc Brooker" year="2015" />.

What we retry depends on what the provider told us:

| Provider response                         | Meaning                       | Action                                     |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Timeout, connection reset                 | Unknown outcome               | Per-category policy (above)                |
| `429` too many requests                   | Throttled                     | Retry with backoff; tell the rate limiter  |
| `5xx`                                     | Provider-side failure         | Retry with backoff                         |
| `400` bad request, payload too large      | Our bug                       | Do not retry; dead-letter and alert        |
| APNs `410`, FCM `UNREGISTERED`            | The token is dead             | Do not retry; delete the token             |

After a fixed number of attempts, or once the notification's TTL passes, the message goes to a <Term id="dead-letter-queue">dead-letter queue</Term>. The DLQ is not a graveyard: it has an owner, an alert on its growth rate, and a replay tool, because the most common cause of dead letters is a bug we fix and then want to re-drive.

**What about ordering?** Users notice when "Your order was delivered" arrives before "Your order shipped". It is tempting to demand global ordering. We do not, for two reasons. First, it is expensive: a single ordered stream cannot be parallelized, and our throughput depends on hundreds of partitions processed independently. Second, it is not achievable end to end anyway, because APNs, FCM, and email providers make no ordering promise across separate requests, and a retry naturally reorders a message behind later ones.

What users actually need is narrower: **the latest state wins**. We get that with three cheap tools:
- Partitioning the request topic by `user_id` keeps one user's notifications in order through the processor, which is where caps and aggregation need it.
- A shared `collapse_key` ("order-8812") makes the newer notification replace the older one on the device, so a late "shipped" cannot sit on top of "delivered".
- Each message carries its creation time, and workers drop a message whose collapse key already has a newer one in the send log.

For a live, ordered stream inside an open app, the right tool is a sequence number per connection, which is how Uber's RAMEN delivers in-app messages <Cite href="https://www.uber.com/blog/real-time-push-platform/" title="Uber's Real-Time Push Platform" author="Uber Engineering" year="2020" />. That is a different system from this one.

### Fan-out and priority without head-of-line blocking [#fan-out-and-priority]

At 9:00 on a Monday, marketing launches a campaign to 50M users. At 9:01, a user tries to log in and waits for their code. If both share a queue, the code sits behind millions of promotions. That is <Term id="head-of-line-blocking">head-of-line blocking</Term>, and it is the most common way notification platforms fail their most important messages.

**Lanes.** We split every channel topic into priority lanes — `high`, `normal`, and `bulk` — each with its own consumer pool, shown in <FigRef id="priority-lanes" />.

<Figure
  id="priority-lanes"
  src="./figures/priority-lanes.svg"
  alt="The notification processor writes to three priority lanes: high for OTP and security, normal for orders and social, and bulk for campaigns. Each lane has its own worker pool. All three pools pass through a shared provider rate limiter before reaching the provider; high and normal pools have a reserved share, the bulk pool uses leftover capacity only."
  caption="Priority lanes. Separate queues and worker pools keep bulk traffic from delaying urgent messages; a shared limiter divides the provider's quota between them."
  width="wide"
/>

Separate topics and pools give isolation in time: a backlog in `bulk` cannot delay a message in `high`. But all three lanes still share one provider quota, so the provider rate limiter divides it. `high` and `normal` get reserved shares; `bulk` gets whatever is left. When a burst of login codes arrives, the campaign slows down automatically. Netflix's RENO system uses the same idea, routing events into separate queues by priority with their own processing clusters <Cite href="https://www.infoq.com/news/2022/03/netflix-reno/" title="Netflix's RENO Keeps Experience Consistent across Devices" author="InfoQ" year="2022" />.

The rate limiter is a token bucket per provider and lane (see [Rate limiting algorithms](/chapters/rate-limiting-algorithms)). With hundreds of workers, a central counter becomes a hot spot, so each worker leases a slice of the budget — "you may send 200 messages per second for the next 10 seconds" — and renews it. The limiter trades a little precision for not putting a network hop in front of every send.

**Campaign fan-out.** A campaign is one request that becomes 50M notifications. We must not expand it into 50M messages in one transaction, and we must survive a crash halfway through without sending anyone two copies. <FigRef id="campaign-fanout" /> shows the flow.

<Figure
  id="campaign-fanout"
  src="./figures/campaign-fanout.svg"
  alt="The campaign service schedules a send at 09:00 local time. The scheduler starts one fan-out job per UTC-offset bucket. Fan-out workers page through the segment store 1,000 user IDs at a time, checkpoint their cursor in a campaign progress store, and publish one message per page to a batch topic. The notification processor expands each page into per-user notifications with an id derived from a hash of the campaign and user, and writes them to the bulk lane."
  caption="Campaign fan-out. Deterministic notification ids make every step safe to repeat."
/>

<Steps>
1. The campaign is scheduled for 09:00 in each recipient's local time.
2. The scheduler splits the audience by UTC offset and starts one fan-out job per offset as its 09:00 arrives. A global campaign becomes a few dozen jobs spread across a day.
3. Each fan-out worker reads the segment in pages of 1,000 user IDs. 50M users is 50,000 pages.
4. After each page, it checkpoints its cursor. A crashed worker resumes from the last checkpoint, not from the beginning.
5. It publishes one message per page to a batch topic — 50,000 messages, not 50M.
6. The processor expands each page into individual notifications. The key move: each `notification_id` is `hash(campaign_id, user_id)`.
7. The resulting notifications enter the bulk lane and go through the same pipeline as everything else.
</Steps>

Because the id is derived rather than random, every repeat of every step produces the *same* notification ids. A page replayed after a crash yields notifications whose ids already exist in the send log, so they are skipped. Deterministic ids turn "exactly once" from a coordination problem into a lookup.

At our 40k msg/s bulk budget, a 50M campaign takes about 21 minutes of send capacity. Time-zone bucketing helps here too: an audience spread across many offsets never needs all 50M sends in the same hour.

<Aside>
Segments are snapshotted when a fan-out job starts. A user who joins the segment ten minutes later is not included, and one who leaves is still sent to — unless they opted out, which the processor checks at send time regardless.
</Aside>

<Callout type="interview">
When the interviewer asks "how do you send to 50M users?", the expected answer is not "a bigger queue". It is batching, checkpointing, deterministic ids for idempotency, and a priority lane so the campaign cannot starve transactional traffic.
</Callout>

### Respecting the human [#respecting-the-human]

Every notification interrupts someone. Send too many and users disable notifications for your app entirely, or mark your email as spam, which hurts delivery for everyone. The processor's most important job is deciding when not to send. <FigRef id="processor-pipeline" /> shows its pipeline.

<Figure
  id="processor-pipeline"
  src="./figures/processor-pipeline.svg"
  alt="Inside the notification processor, a request passes through seven stages in order: deduplicate, preferences and opt-outs, quiet hours and send-at, frequency caps, aggregate, select channels, and render templates, before reaching the channel topics. Duplicates, opted-out requests, and over-cap requests go to a suppression log. Requests in quiet hours or waiting for a digest window are handed to a scheduler."
  caption="The processor pipeline. Cheap checks that can drop a notification run first; expensive rendering runs last."
/>

1. **Deduplicate.** Drop a notification whose `notification_id` was already processed, or whose `(user, collapse_key)` matches one sent in the last few minutes.
2. **Preferences and opt-outs.** Look up the user's matrix of category × channel. "Order updates: push yes, email yes, SMS no." Some categories, such as security alerts, cannot be disabled; preferences can only narrow their channels.
3. **Quiet hours and send-at.** If it is 02:00 for the user, a social notification is handed to the scheduler to be released when quiet hours end — or dropped if its TTL will expire first. High-priority categories bypass quiet hours.
4. **Frequency caps.** Per-user counters per category and window, such as "at most 3 marketing pushes per day". Counters live in an in-memory store with expiring keys. Over-cap notifications are suppressed, not delayed.
5. **Aggregate.** Ten "someone liked your photo" events in five minutes should become one notification: "Priya and 9 others liked your photo." The processor holds aggregatable notifications in a short window keyed by `(user, aggregation_key)` and emits a digest when the window closes. This trades a few minutes of latency for far fewer interruptions.
6. **Select channels.** Among the channels the user allows, pick the cheapest one that will work: in-app if the app is open, push if the user has a valid device token, email otherwise, and SMS only for categories that require it.
7. **Render.** Fill the localized template with the user's data, enforce size limits (an APNs payload is limited to 4 KB), and escape all user data in HTML email.

Frequency caps are the stage most often built wrong, usually by counting with a read followed by a separate write, which lets two concurrent notifications both see "2 of 3" and both send. The counter must be a single atomic operation:

```ts title="frequency-cap.ts"
// One counter per user, category, channel, and UTC day; the key expires on its own.
export async function withinCap(n: Notification, limit: number): Promise<boolean> {
  const day = new Date().toISOString().slice(0, 10);
  const key = `cap:${n.userId}:${n.category}:${n.channel}:${day}`;
  const count = await kv.incr(key);             // atomic increment
  if (count === 1) await kv.expire(key, 2 * 86_400);
  return count <= limit;
}
```

Because all of one user's notifications are processed on the same partition, these counters can also live in the processor's local state, avoiding a network call entirely; the remote version above is the simpler starting point. Note that a notification counts against the cap when it is *decided*, not when it is delivered. A notification that later fails at the provider has still used a slot, which errs on the side of sending less.

Every suppression is recorded with its reason. When a producer asks "why didn't my user get this?", the answer should be one query away: "suppressed: frequency cap, marketing push, 3 of 3 used today."

LinkedIn's Air Traffic Controller is a public example of this design: a single gateway that decides channel, timing, aggregation, and filtering for every member notification, built as a stream processor partitioned by member id with local state <Cite href="https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/messaging-notifications/air-traffic-controller-member-first-notifications-at-linkedin" title="Air Traffic Controller: Member-first notifications at LinkedIn" author="Changji Shi, Adriel Fuad" year="2018" />. Partitioning by user is what makes the caps and aggregation windows cheap: all of one user's notifications arrive at the same processor instance, so its counters can be local.

**When the preference service is down.** The processor cannot check opt-outs. Should it send anyway? The answer depends on the category, and the asymmetry is the point:

- **Marketing fails closed.** Sending a promotion to someone who opted out damages trust, can breach consent rules, and risks spam complaints. Hold bulk traffic until preferences are back; nobody misses a delayed promotion.
- **Transactional fails open, using the last cached preferences.** A login code that never arrives locks a user out. A stale cache entry is far better than no code.

<Callout type="tip" title="Write the failure policy into the category">
Make "fail open" or "fail closed", "retry on unknown outcome", quiet-hour bypass, and default TTL properties of each notification category, reviewed when the category is created. Then policy is data, not scattered `if` statements.
</Callout>

### Living with providers [#living-with-providers]

APNs, FCM, the email provider, and the SMS provider are the least reliable, least controllable part of our system, and every message goes through one of them. We wrap each in a **provider adapter**, shown in <FigRef id="provider-adapter" />.

<Figure
  id="provider-adapter"
  src="./figures/provider-adapter.svg"
  alt="A channel worker sends through a provider adapter containing a rate limiter followed by a circuit breaker. With the breaker closed, requests go to the primary provider; with it open, they fail over to a secondary provider. Both providers send delivery webhooks to a tracking service, which records state in a status store. Separately, the worker deletes tokens from the device registry when a provider reports them invalid."
  caption="A provider adapter: rate limit, circuit breaker, failover, and a feedback loop that keeps the device registry clean."
/>

**Rate limits are someone else's numbers.** The adapter's limiter is configured from the provider's published quotas, not from our wishes. For FCM that means the project-wide per-minute quota (and a per-device limit: on Android, a burst of up to 240 messages per minute and 5,000 per hour to one device) <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/throttling-and-quotas" title="FCM throttling and quotas" author="Firebase" />. For SMS it means per-sender throughput. When a provider returns `429`, the adapter tightens its own budget rather than retrying harder.

**Circuit breakers and bulkheads.** Recall the concurrency estimate: a provider slowing from 100 ms to 1 s needs ten times the in-flight requests. Without protection, workers pile up waiting on the sick provider. Each adapter therefore has its own connection pool (a bulkhead) and a <Term id="circuit-breaker">circuit breaker</Term>: after a threshold of failures or timeouts it opens, fails new calls immediately for a cool-down period, then lets a few trial requests through <Cite href="https://martinfowler.com/bliki/CircuitBreaker.html" title="CircuitBreaker" author="Martin Fowler" />. Failing fast keeps workers free and turns a provider brownout into a clean, visible backlog.

**Failover where it exists.** Email and SMS are commodities: we can contract two providers and fail over when the breaker opens. Push is not. There is exactly one way to reach an iPhone's notification center, and it is APNs. When APNs is down, we queue (within each notification's TTL) and deliver the important updates through the in-app inbox when users open the app. Netflix's RENO combines best-effort pushes to online devices with periodic pulls by the app for this reason <Cite href="https://www.infoq.com/news/2022/03/netflix-reno/" title="Netflix's RENO Keeps Experience Consistent across Devices" author="InfoQ" year="2022" />.

**Token hygiene.** Device tokens change when apps are reinstalled or restored, and die when apps are deleted. APNs answers a request for a dead token with HTTP `410` and reason `Unregistered` <Cite href="https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/handling-notification-responses-from-apns" title="Handling notification responses from APNs" author="Apple" />; FCM's HTTP v1 API returns `UNREGISTERED`. The adapter deletes those tokens through the registry's token index. Firebase also recommends treating tokens as stale when a device has not been active for a long period — over a month in their guidance — and pruning or refreshing them proactively, because sends to stale tokens waste quota and make delivery metrics look worse than they are <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/manage-tokens" title="Best practices for FCM registration token management" author="Firebase" />. Our `last_seen` column exists for exactly this.

**Webhooks are a distributed system too.** Provider callbacks are themselves delivered at least once and in no particular order: a "delivered" event can arrive before our own worker has recorded "sent", and the same "bounced" event can arrive three times. The tracking service therefore treats status as a one-way state machine (<FigRef id="notification-lifecycle" />). It applies an event only if it moves the delivery forward, so a late "sent" never overwrites "delivered", and a repeated event changes nothing. Webhook endpoints verify each provider's signature, acknowledge quickly, and push events onto a topic for processing, because a provider that sees slow responses from us will retry and make the backlog worse.

**Email deliverability is a reputation system.** Mailbox providers judge every sender. Gmail requires senders of marketing and subscribed messages to support one-click unsubscribe, and asks bulk senders to keep their user-reported spam rate below 0.30%, recommending below 0.10% <Cite href="https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126" title="Email sender guidelines" author="Google" />. One-click unsubscribe is standardized by the `List-Unsubscribe` and `List-Unsubscribe-Post: List-Unsubscribe=One-Click` headers <Cite href="https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8058" title="RFC 8058: Signaling One-Click Functionality for List Email Headers" author="J. Levine, T. Herkula" year="2017" />. Email providers watch the same signals: Amazon SES places an account under review when its bounce rate reaches 5% and may pause sending at 10% <Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ses/latest/dg/reputationdashboardmessages.html" title="Reputation metrics messages" author="Amazon Web Services" />.

For our design, that means three concrete mechanisms:
- Bounce and complaint webhooks flow into the tracking service, which immediately suppresses the address for future sends.
- The unsubscribe endpoint writes straight to preferences, and the processor honors it on the next message.
- Transactional and marketing email go out from separate sending domains or IP pools, so a bad campaign cannot hurt password resets.

<Callout type="warning">
Deliverability problems are invisible to your own success metrics. The provider accepted the email, and it still went to spam. Track complaint and bounce rates per sending domain as first-class service-level indicators.
</Callout>

## Scaling and bottlenecks

Start from the peak of 58k msg/s and ask what breaks first.

**At today's peak:**
- **Request topic.** Assume one processor instance handles about 1k notifications per second including lookups. That is about 60 instances at peak; we provision 128 partitions so we can double consumers without repartitioning.
- **Lookups.** 58k preference reads and 58k device reads per second. Both are served from cache; at a 95% hit rate the databases see about 2.9k reads per second each.
- **Status writes.** 232k writes per second is the largest load in the system. We write only state transitions, batch them per partition, and let the wide-column store absorb them.
- **FCM quota.** The 1.04M per-minute Android peak needs a quota increase that must be requested and grown in advance. Until then, the bulk lane absorbs the shortfall by slowing down.

<Callout type="interview" title="What breaks first?">
When asked how the design scales, do not say "add more servers". Name the first real limit with a number: here it is a provider quota, not anything we run. Then name the second (status writes) and what you would do about each.
</Callout>

**At ×10 (580k msg/s, 10B notifications a day):**
- Processors, channel workers, and topics scale horizontally; they are stateless apart from per-user counters, which move with the partitions.
- Provider quotas dominate. Android peak becomes about 10.4M messages per minute, more than 17 times FCM's default quota, reachable only through a long series of increases planned with the provider. SMS needs many dedicated senders and usually several providers.
- Aggregation and caps become *capacity* features, as well as user-experience features: every notification we merge or suppress is one we do not pay to send.

**At ×100:**
- Android peak passes 100M messages per minute. Firebase asks for at least 30 days' notice for quota requests above 18M messages per minute <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/throttling-and-quotas" title="FCM throttling and quotas" author="Firebase" />, so capacity planning with providers becomes a standing process, not an event.
- A single region becomes the bottleneck and the risk. We run the platform in several regions, route each user to a home region by `user_id`, and replicate preferences and device data across regions.
- Campaigns are no longer something the platform can push "as fast as possible". They become scheduled workloads with planned capacity, like batch jobs on a shared cluster.

## Reliability and failure modes

| Component                     | Failure                                  | Detection                               | Mitigation                                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Push, email, or SMS provider  | Outage or 5xx                            | Error rate per adapter; breaker opens   | Fail fast; retry with backoff within TTL; fail over for email and SMS      |
| Provider                      | Slow responses                           | Latency p99 per adapter                 | Timeouts, bulkheads, breaker; never let one provider consume all workers   |
| Request topic                 | Broker or partition loss                 | Under-replicated partitions; produce errors | Replication factor 3, `acks=all`; the API returns `503` and producers retry |
| Processor or worker           | Crash mid-batch                          | Consumer lag; restarts                  | Replay from last committed offset; send log skips duplicates               |
| Any consumer                  | Poison message (bad template, bad data)  | Repeated failures on one message        | Bounded retries, then dead-letter; never block the partition               |
| Preference service            | Unavailable                              | Lookup errors                           | Marketing fails closed; transactional fails open with cached preferences   |
| A producer                    | Runaway loop sends millions of requests  | Per-producer rate and anomaly alerts    | Per-producer quotas at the API; per-user caps in the processor             |
| Device registry               | Dead tokens accumulate                   | Rising `410` and `UNREGISTERED` rates   | Delete on response; prune stale tokens by `last_seen`                       |
| Region                        | Region loss                              | Health checks                           | Users fail over to a secondary region; ingestion fails over first           |

The runaway producer deserves a special mention because it is the incident almost every notification team has: a bug in someone else's service sends the same notification in a loop. The idempotency key does not help if the bug generates a new key each time. Per-producer quotas at the API, per-user caps in the processor, and a large red "pause this category" switch for on-call engineers are what stop a bug from reaching millions of phones.

## Observability

We measure the system the way users experience it: per priority lane, per channel, per provider.

**Service-level objectives:**
- 99.99% of API requests succeed (excluding producer errors).
- High lane: p99 time from acceptance to provider handoff under 5 s.
- Normal lane: p99 under 60 s. Bulk lane: campaigns finish within their planned window.

**Key metrics and alerts:**
- **Consumer lag per topic and lane**, in seconds rather than messages. Lag in `high` pages someone; lag in `bulk` during a campaign is expected.
- **Provider outcomes by status code.** A jump in `429` means a quota problem; a jump in `410` means a token-hygiene problem; a jump in `400` means we shipped a bug.
- **Suppression rate by reason.** A sudden rise in "frequency cap" suppressions usually means a producer changed behavior.
- **DLQ growth rate**, with an owner.
- **Email complaint and bounce rates** per sending domain, alerting well before provider thresholds.
- **SMS spend per day**, because a bug in SMS routing is the most expensive bug in the system.

**Tracing.** The `notification_id` is the correlation id from the producer's request through every topic, worker, and provider call. We also log the provider's own message id (for APNs, the `apns-id` response header) so a single notification can be traced with the provider's support team.

## Security and privacy

- **Producer authentication and authorization.** Producers authenticate with service identities (mutual TLS or signed tokens). Each producer may send only the categories it owns, so the marketing service cannot send "security alert" notifications that bypass opt-outs.
- **Template injection.** User-supplied data is always escaped when rendered, especially in HTML email. Templates are reviewed like code.
- **The lock screen is public.** Push content can appear on a locked phone in a meeting room. Never put sensitive details — balances, medical information, message bodies from sensitive conversations — in the push text. Send "You have a new message" and let the app fetch details after the user unlocks it.
- **Data minimization and retention.** Template parameters often contain personal data. We keep them only as long as status queries need them, keep long-term analytics on ids and outcomes, and let row TTLs delete the rest.
- **Account deletion.** Deleting a user removes their devices, preferences, inbox, and pending scheduled notifications.
- **Consent records.** For marketing email and SMS, we store when and how each user opted in. Consent rules vary by country and channel; the platform's job is to make the consent record easy to keep and impossible to bypass.
- **Provider credentials.** APNs signing keys, FCM service accounts, and email and SMS API keys live in a secrets manager and are rotated. Only the channel adapters can read them.

## Trade-offs and alternatives

<Tradeoffs>
| Decision                    | Chosen                                    | Alternative                         | Why                                                                                     |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| API contract                | `202` after durable append                | Synchronous send                    | Producers get fast, reliable acceptance; delivery problems become queue lag, not errors |
| Delivery semantics          | At-least-once + dedup at three layers     | Exactly-once                        | Exactly-once cannot extend past the provider boundary; dedup is cheaper and honest      |
| Queue                       | Log-based broker partitioned by user      | Per-message queue (SQS-style)       | Replay, per-user ordering, and local per-user state for caps and aggregation            |
| Priority                    | Separate lanes and worker pools           | One queue with priority field       | Isolation: a backlog in bulk cannot block the high lane                                 |
| Retries                     | Delay topics with backoff and jitter      | Sleep and retry in the worker       | Sleeping blocks every message behind it in the partition                                |
| Campaign fan-out            | Paged batches + deterministic ids         | Expand all recipients up front      | Crash-safe and resumable; replays are free                                              |
| Notification storage        | Wide-column store, TTL, partitioned by user | Relational with indexes           | Write volume and automatic expiry at 1B/day; relational is right for v1                 |
| Preference outage           | Fail closed for marketing, open for transactional | One policy for all          | The cost of a wrong send and of a missed send differ by category                        |
| Provider redundancy         | Secondary providers for email and SMS     | Single provider per channel         | Commodity channels make failover cheap; push has no alternative                         |
| Aggregation                 | Short hold window for digests             | Send every event immediately        | Fewer interruptions and lower cost for a few minutes of latency                         |
</Tradeoffs>

## Evolution

Nobody builds the final design first. Here is how we would get there.

**v1 — a small team, a few million notifications a day.**
- One notification service with the same public API.
- A relational `notifications` table doubles as the queue: workers claim rows with `SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`.
- One provider per channel, preferences in the same database, retries with backoff, no lanes.
- This is boring and correct. The API contract — `202`, idempotency keys, categories, TTLs — is the part that must be right from day one, because producers will depend on it for years.

**v2 — the platform.**
- Move to a log-based broker with per-channel topics and priority lanes.
- Add the send log, delay topics, and a DLQ with a replay tool.
- Add the processor pipeline: preferences cache, quiet hours, caps, and campaign fan-out.
- Add the tracking service and provider webhooks, so status means "delivered", not "we called someone".

**v3 — global scale.**
- Run in multiple regions with users homed by id.
- Add secondary email and SMS providers with automatic failover.
- Add learned send-time optimization and relevance models that decide whether a notification is worth sending at all — the direction LinkedIn describes for its platform.
- Add a persistent connection channel for real-time in-app delivery. Uber's RAMEN platform shows the shape: at-least-once delivery over long-lived connections with sequence numbers and acknowledgments, at more than 1.5M concurrent connections <Cite href="https://www.uber.com/blog/real-time-push-platform/" title="Uber's Real-Time Push Platform" author="Uber Engineering" year="2020" />.

## Summary

| Requirement                         | How the design meets it                                                              |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1B/day, 58k msg/s peak              | Thin API, partitioned topics, stateless workers that scale horizontally              |
| Never lose an accepted notification | `202` only after a replicated append; outbox for producers; every path ends in a recorded state |
| Rarely duplicate                    | Idempotency keys, send log, collapse ids, per-category retry policy                  |
| Login codes in under 5 s at p99     | Priority lanes, reserved provider share, separate worker pools                       |
| 50M-user campaigns                  | Paged fan-out, checkpoints, deterministic ids, bulk lane                             |
| Respect users                       | Preferences, quiet hours, frequency caps, digests, one-click unsubscribe             |
| Survive providers                   | Rate limits from real quotas, circuit breakers, failover, token hygiene              |

- Accept fast and durably; deliver asynchronously.
- Assume at-least-once everywhere and make duplicates detectable or harmless.
- Isolate by priority and by channel so one backlog cannot block another.
- Put policy in one place — the processor — and make suppression explainable.
- Design around provider limits, because they are the real ceiling of the system.
- Start with a relational table as a queue; earn the broker, the lanes, and the regions.
