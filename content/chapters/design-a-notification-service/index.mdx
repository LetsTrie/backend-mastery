Every product eventually needs to tap someone on the shoulder. Your order shipped. Someone replied to your comment. Here is your login code. It looks like the easiest feature in the backlog: call Apple, call Google, call an email provider, done.

Then the product grows, and one bad Monday morning everything goes wrong at once. A marketing campaign to 40 million people delays password-reset emails by an hour. A retry loop sends the same "Your payment failed" push four times. A user who turned off promotional email gets one anyway and reports it as spam, which hurts delivery for everyone. And the push provider starts rejecting requests because of a quota nobody knew existed.

In this chapter we design a notification service that prevents all four. It sends a billion notifications a day by push, email, SMS, and an in-app inbox, and it knows when *not* to send: when the user opted out, when it is 3 a.m. for them, or when they already got the same message. The last section walks through that Monday again and shows which part of the design stops each failure.

## Problem and scope

We are building an internal platform. Other teams' services, which we call **producers**, ask it to notify a user about something. The platform decides whether, when, and how to deliver that notification, hands it to the right third-party provider, and tracks what happened. The platform covers:

- Channels: iOS push through <Term id="apns">Apple Push Notification service (APNs)</Term>, Android push through <Term id="fcm">Firebase Cloud Messaging (FCM)</Term>, email, SMS, and an in-app inbox.
- Two traffic shapes: **transactional** notifications for one user ("your code is 482913") and **bulk** campaigns to a segment of users ("20% off this weekend").
- User preferences per category and channel, quiet hours, and time zones.
- Templates with localization and personalization.
- Scheduling, including "deliver at 9:00 in each user's local time".
- <Term id="frequency-cap">Frequency caps</Term>, <Term id="deduplication">deduplication</Term>, <Term id="channel-escalation">escalation across channels</Term>, and <Term id="delivery-tracking">delivery tracking</Term>.

## Requirements

### Functional

1. A producer can send a notification to one user, either by calling an API or by publishing an event.
2. A producer can launch a campaign to a segment of up to 50M users, immediately or on a schedule.
3. Users control which categories they receive on which channels, set quiet hours, and have a time zone. An opt-out takes effect for every notification not yet handed to a provider, including ones already queued or scheduled.
4. Notifications are rendered from versioned, localized templates with per-user data.
5. Producers can query the delivery status of any notification they sent; users can page through their in-app inbox.
6. The platform enforces per-user frequency caps. It recognizes and drops deterministic internal replays, and keeps the duplicates a user can see rare.

### Non-functional

- **Throughput:** 1B notifications per day, with peaks around 58k notifications per second and about 63k provider calls per second (derived below).
- **Latency:** for high-priority notifications (login codes, security alerts), <Term id="p99">p99</Term> under 5 s from API acceptance to provider handoff. Bulk notifications are best-effort and may take minutes.
- **Availability:** the ingestion API is 99.99% available (≈ 53 min of downtime a year). Delivery may lag during incidents, but accepting requests must not stop.
- **Durability:** once we return `202 Accepted`, the notification is never silently lost. It is delivered, suppressed by a rule, expired, dead-lettered, or marked indeterminate, and every outcome is recorded. The one exception is losing an entire region, where notifications accepted in the last few seconds may be lost or sent twice; [Regions](#regions) quantifies that.
- **Delivery semantics:** <Term id="at-least-once-delivery">at-least-once</Term> internally, with deduplication at every layer so that users rarely see duplicates.

Notice what is *not* on the list: exactly-once delivery and global ordering. We will see why both are expensive and why users do not need them.

## Back-of-the-envelope estimation

We size the system in five steps: traffic, the provider calls that traffic turns into, storage, concurrency, and cost. Each assumption sits right next to the calculation that uses it. The numbers are deliberately round; the goal is the right order of magnitude, not false precision.

### Traffic

Assume 200 million <Term id="dau">daily active users (DAU)</Term>, each receiving 5 notifications a day on average.

- Notifications per day: 200M users × 5 = 1 billion
- Average rate: 1B ÷ 86,400 seconds in a day ≈ 11.6k per second

Traffic is never flat. Morning commutes, campaign launches, and live events bunch it up, so assume the peak is five times the average.

- Peak rate: 11.6k × 5 ≈ 58k per second

Each notification goes out on one channel. Assume 60% are push, 25% in-app, 13% email, and 2% SMS.

- Push: 1B × 60% = 600M per day
- In-app: 1B × 25% = 250M per day
- Email: 1B × 13% = 130M per day
- SMS: 1B × 2% = 20M per day

### From notifications to provider calls

Fifty-eight thousand notifications per second is not the number our providers see. A push goes to every device the user has, and some provider calls fail and are retried. Assume a push reaches 1.5 devices on average (some users have a phone and a tablet; some devices have no valid token), half of those devices run Android, and 3% of provider calls are retried. At peak:

- Push notifications: 58k × 60% ≈ 34.7k per second
- Push device sends: 34.7k × 1.5 devices ≈ 52k per second
- Android device sends: 52k × 50% × 60 seconds ≈ 1.56M per minute
- Email sends: 58k × 13% ≈ 7.5k per second
- SMS sends: 58k × 2% ≈ 1.2k per second
- In-app inbox writes, to our own store rather than a provider: 58k × 25% ≈ 14.5k per second
- Provider calls: 52k push + 7.5k email + 1.2k SMS ≈ 61k per second
- Provider attempts, counting retries: 61k × 1.03 ≈ 63k per second

Sizing workers from the 58k figure would under-provision them by 8%. Sizing the FCM quota from it would be off by a third. Two of these numbers should make you stop.

First, **1.56M Android messages per minute**. FCM's HTTP v1 API enforces a per-project quota that counts messages, not requests, and the default is 600k messages per minute <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/throttling-and-quotas" title="FCM throttling and quotas" author="Firebase" />. Our peak is 2.6 times that. A standing increase is at most 25% per request, and only when usage has regularly reached 80% of the current quota and the client error ratio stays under 5%. From 600k, that is five consecutive increases (600k → 750k → 938k → 1.17M → 1.46M → 1.83M), each earned by running close to the previous limit. This is a capacity-planning conversation that starts months before launch, not on launch day.

Second, **1.2k SMS per second**. SMS throughput is allocated per sender. Twilio's documented defaults are 3 <Term id="sms-segment">message segments</Term> per second for a US toll-free number and 100 per second for a short code <Cite href="https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/115002943027-Understanding-Twilio-Rate-Limits-and-Message-Queues" title="Understanding Twilio rate limits and message queues" author="Twilio" />. Our peak is about 12 short codes' worth of default throughput.

### Storage

We store six kinds of data, each with its own size and lifetime.

**Notification records.** One record per notification holds its ids, category, template version, parameters, and status: about 500 bytes. We keep records for 90 days, for status queries and history.

- Per day: 1B × 500 bytes = 500 GB
- For 90 days: 500 GB × 90 = 45 TB, or 135 TB with three replicas

**Status events.** A channel delivery moves through up to four states that a caller can see:

1. **Queued:** accepted and waiting in a channel topic.
2. **Sent:** handed to the provider, which accepted it.
3. **Delivered:** the provider, carrier, or mail server confirmed receipt.
4. **Opened:** the user tapped the push, read the inbox item, or opened the email.

A failure replaces the later states with *failed*, *bounced*, or *expired*, so a delivery still records at most four events. Push channels often produce fewer because they lack per-message delivery receipts, while the rare notification that escalates to a second channel creates a second delivery. We use four events per notification as a conservative rounded ceiling for capacity planning and measure the actual delivery multiplier in production. Each event is about 100 bytes.

- Conservative ceiling per day: 1B notifications × 4 events × 100 bytes = 400 GB

**The send log.** Before every provider call, a worker writes one row per *target*: one per device for push, one per address for email and SMS. That row is how it tells a safe retry from a duplicate (see [reliable delivery](#reliable-delivery)). A row holds the notification id, the target, its state, an attempt id, and a <Term id="lease">lease</Term>: about 100 bytes.

- Rows per day: 600M push × 1.5 devices = 900M, plus 130M email and 20M SMS = 1.05B rows
- Rows only need to outlive retries, which stop when a notification's <Term id="ttl">time-to-live (TTL)</Term> passes. Most TTLs are a day or less, and a row is kept one more day as a safety margin, so a row lives about two days: 1.05B rows × 100 bytes × 2 days ≈ 210 GB

**Device registry.** Assume 500M registered users with 2 devices each, at about 200 bytes per device.

- 500M × 2 × 200 bytes = 200 GB

**Preferences.** About 1 KB per user.

- 500M × 1 KB = 500 GB

**In-app inbox.** 250M in-app notifications a day, kept for 30 days, at about 500 bytes each.

- 250M × 30 days × 500 bytes = 3.75 TB

Storage is large but unremarkable. The device registry and preferences fit comfortably in memory across a cache cluster, which matters because we read them for every notification.

### Concurrency

<Term id="littles-law">Little's law</Term> says the number of requests in flight equals the arrival rate times how long each request takes. Assume a provider call takes about 100 ms end to end.

- In flight at peak: 63k per second × 0.1 s ≈ 6.3k requests
- If providers slow to 1 s: 63k per second × 1 s ≈ 63k requests

A provider that slows from 100 ms to 1 s multiplies our required concurrency by ten. A slow provider is often more dangerous than a dead one, because a dead one fails fast. We return to this in [Living with providers](#living-with-providers).

Status updates, not notifications, are our heaviest write path. Using the conservative ceiling above:

- Status writes at peak: 58k notifications per second × 4 events ≈ 232k writes per second

[Scaling and bottlenecks](#scaling-and-bottlenecks) sizes the store for it. Finally, campaigns get a bulk budget of 40k notifications per second at peak, which leaves the rest of the capacity for transactional traffic.

- A 50M-user campaign: 50M ÷ 40k per second = 1,250 seconds ≈ 21 minutes

### Cost

Assume list prices: about $0.01 per SMS segment including carrier fees (Twilio's US base rate is $0.0083 per segment before them <Cite href="https://www.twilio.com/en-us/sms/pricing/us" title="SMS pricing in the United States" author="Twilio" />), $0.10 per 1,000 emails (Amazon SES's list price <Cite href="https://aws.amazon.com/ses/pricing/" title="Amazon SES pricing" author="Amazon Web Services" />), and nothing per push message.

- SMS: 20M × $0.01 ≈ $200k per day, or about $73M per year
- Email: 130M ÷ 1,000 × $0.10 ≈ $13k per day
- Push: APNs and FCM charge nothing per message, so $0 beyond our own servers

This changes the design more than any other number. SMS is 2% of our volume and about 94% of our provider spend ($200k of $213k a day). Real contracts are cheaper at this volume, but the ratio holds: every category that uses SMS must justify it, the channel-selection logic prefers push and in-app wherever the user allows them, and daily SMS spend gets an alert like any other service-level indicator. It also makes SMS the most attractive target for fraud, which we cover in [Security and privacy](#security-and-privacy).

## API design

Producers talk to us in two ways. Synchronous HTTP suits request-driven flows such as login codes. Asynchronous events suit flows that already publish domain events ("order shipped"). Both paths land in the same request topics, so everything downstream is identical.

### Send one notification

```http title="Request"
POST /v1/notifications
Authorization: Bearer <service token>
Idempotency-Key: order-8812-shipped
Content-Type: application/json

{
  "recipient": { "user_id": "u_51f2c9" },
  "category": "order_updates",
  "template": "order_shipped",
  "data": { "order_id": "8812", "eta": "2026-10-27" },
  "priority": "normal",
  "channels": "auto",
  "ttl_seconds": 86400,
  "collapse_key": "order-8812"
}
```

```http title="Response"
HTTP/1.1 202 Accepted
Content-Type: application/json

{ "notification_id": "01JB7Q4ZK3N0X8Y5V2M6T9RWHF", "status": "accepted" }
```

A few deliberate choices here:

- **`202 Accepted`, not `200 OK`.** We have recorded the request, not delivered it. Pretending otherwise invites producers to build on a guarantee we cannot make.
- **`Idempotency-Key` header.** If the producer times out and retries, we return the original `notification_id` instead of creating a second notification. The <Term id="idempotency-key">idempotency key</Term> follows the IETF draft for an `Idempotency-Key` header <Cite href="https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-httpapi-idempotency-key-header/" title="The Idempotency-Key HTTP Header Field" author="IETF HTTPAPI Working Group" />; the general technique gets its own chapter in Building blocks.
- **`category`, not channel.** Producers say *what kind* of notification this is. Users' preferences are expressed per category, and the platform picks channels. A producer can request specific channels, but `auto` is the default. A category also carries policy: its priority, whether it may be disabled, what to do on an unknown outcome, and whether its content is sensitive.
- **`ttl_seconds`.** A "your driver is arriving" push is worthless ten minutes later. Every notification carries a TTL, and stale ones are dropped rather than delivered late.
- **`collapse_key`.** Later notifications with the same key replace earlier ones on the device, so "order shipped" can be replaced by "out for delivery" instead of stacking.

### The rest of the surface

| Endpoint                                     | Purpose                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `POST /v1/campaigns`                         | Send a template to a segment, now or at a scheduled local time   |
| `GET /v1/notifications/{id}`                 | Per-channel status: queued, sent, delivered, failed, suppressed, indeterminate |
| `PUT /v1/users/{id}/preferences`             | Opt in or out per category × channel; quiet hours; time zone     |
| `POST /v1/users/{id}/devices`                | Register or refresh a <Term id="device-token">device token</Term> |
| `DELETE /v1/users/{id}/devices/{device_id}`  | Unregister on logout                                             |
| `GET /v1/users/{id}/inbox?cursor=…&limit=50` | In-app inbox, newest first, with `next_cursor` and unread count  |
| `POST /v1/users/{id}/inbox/{nid}/read`       | Mark one notification read; updates the unread count and badge   |
| `POST /internal/providers/{name}/events`     | Webhooks from providers: delivered, bounced, complained, STOP    |

Campaigns return a `campaign_id` and are always asynchronous. Status queries are eventually consistent: a notification may show `queued` for a moment after the push has already arrived. That is acceptable for a status page and keeps the write path cheap.

The inbox uses **cursor pagination**. The cursor encodes the last row's `(created_at, notification_id)`, so a page request is a single range read that stays correct while new notifications arrive at the top. Offset pagination would skip or repeat rows whenever the inbox grows between page loads, which on a busy account is always.

Two smaller points. First, `recipient` can also be a raw address (`{ "email": "…" }`) for flows that happen before a user exists, such as invitations. Those bypass per-user preferences, because there is no user, but they still obey the global suppression list of bounced, complaining, and unsubscribed addresses. Second, the API enforces per-producer quotas and answers `429 Too Many Requests` when a producer exceeds them. That is the platform's <Term id="backpressure">backpressure</Term> toward producers: a runaway loop is throttled at the door rather than absorbed into the queue.

## Data model

Our access patterns drive the storage choices:

1. Write a notification record — 1B times a day, rarely updated afterwards.
2. Look up a notification by id for status queries.
3. Read a user's in-app inbox, newest first, one page at a time.
4. Read a user's devices and preferences — on every single notification.
5. Claim a send, per notification and per target, before every provider call.
6. Find a device by its token when a provider says the token is dead.

<FigRef id="data-model" /> shows the core tables.

**Identity and ordering are separate columns.** A `notification_id` is an opaque string with one job: identity. For API requests it is a ULID (a 128-bit, lexicographically sortable unique identifier); for campaign notifications it is `hash(campaign_id, user_id)`, which is deliberately not sortable (see [Fan-out and priority](#fan-out-and-priority)). Because the two kinds of id cannot be ordered against each other, every table that needs time order carries an explicit `created_at` and clusters on `(created_at, notification_id)`. The id breaks ties; it never carries meaning.

**Notifications** are write-heavy, append-mostly, time-ordered per user, and expire after 90 days. That is the textbook fit for a <Term id="wide-column-store">wide-column store</Term> such as Cassandra: <Term id="partition">partition</Term> by `user_id`, cluster by `created_at` descending, and set a row TTL so old data deletes itself. Each user's partition stays small: 5 notifications a day for 90 days is 450 rows, about 225 KB. A second table keyed by `notification_id` alone serves status lookups.

**Deliveries** record one row per notification per channel: its state, attempt count, and timestamps. This is where up to 232k status writes per second land under our conservative ceiling.

**The send log** records one row per notification per *target* — a device for push, an address for email and SMS — and is the guard in front of every provider call. Its lease and epoch columns are explained in [reliable delivery](#reliable-delivery) and [Regions](#regions). It needs a cheap conditional write (compare-and-set), and its rows expire a day after the notification's TTL.

**The inbox is its own table.** It is tempting to serve the inbox by filtering `notifications_by_user` to in-app notifications, but a wide-column store cannot filter a partition by a non-key column efficiently: it would read every row and discard most. So the in-app worker writes a projection, `inbox_by_user`, with the rendered title and body and a `read_at` column. A page is a range read on the clustering key, exactly what the cursor encodes. The durable read state is `read_at`; the per-user `unread_count` in the cache is an optimization, incremented when a row is written and decremented on mark-read, and recomputed from the table whenever the app opens.

**Devices** are keyed by `user_id`. When APNs reports a token as unregistered, we must find the device without knowing the user. A secondary index on a billion high-cardinality tokens is the wrong tool in a wide-column store, so we keep a lookup table, `device_by_token`, keyed by a hash of the token and written in the same request that registers the device.

**Preferences** are small, read on every notification, and rarely written. They live in a durable store with a cache in front; a 95% cache hit rate turns 58k reads per second into about 2.9k database reads.

**Templates** are versioned and localized, and they are few and edited by humans, so a relational database is the right home. Workers cache compiled templates in memory.

**Pin the template version at acceptance.** A notification accepted at 09:00 against template v7 and rendered at 09:03, after v8 deployed, should still render v7. Otherwise a campaign in flight changes wording halfway through, and a status query can no longer say what the user saw. The processor resolves `template` to a specific version the first time it sees a request and stores that version in the record.

## High-level design

Start with the simplest thing that could work, shown in <FigRef id="naive-design" />: the order service calls each provider directly when an order ships.

<Figure
  id="naive-design"
  src="./figures/naive-design.svg"
  alt="An order service makes synchronous HTTPS calls directly to APNs, FCM, an email provider, and an SMS provider; the SMS call is highlighted as failing."
  caption="The naive design. Every producer integrates with every provider, synchronously."
/>

It works for a demo and fails in production in at least five ways:

1. **Latency leaks into the product.** The checkout request now waits on four external calls. When the SMS provider takes 3 s, checkout takes 3 s.
2. **Failures leak too.** If the SMS call fails, does the order fail? Retrying inline blocks the request; not retrying loses the message.
3. **Every team reimplements everything.** Device tokens, preferences, templates, quiet hours, retries, and provider credentials end up copied into every service, each copy slightly different.
4. **Nobody sees the whole picture.** Ten teams each send "reasonable" volumes, and together they send a user 40 notifications a day. LinkedIn built its Air Traffic Controller as a single decision point for every member notification after members told them they were getting too many <Cite href="https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/messaging-notifications/air-traffic-controller-member-first-notifications-at-linkedin" title="Air Traffic Controller: Member-first notifications at LinkedIn" author="Changji Shi, Adriel Fuad" year="2018" />.
5. **Provider limits are shared but enforced nowhere.** The FCM quota is per project, not per team.

A tempting middle ground is a shared client library: every team imports one well-written `notify()` function. It fixes the duplicated code in point 3 and nothing else. The calls are still synchronous inside each producer, the frequency caps still cannot see across teams, and the provider quota is still consumed by whoever happens to be busiest. Problems 1, 2, 4, and 5 are all about *shared state and shared capacity*, and a library cannot own either. A service can.

The fix is a dedicated platform with queues at its front door. <FigRef id="high-level-design" /> shows the design we will refine for the rest of the chapter.

<Steps>
1. A producer calls `POST /v1/notifications` (or publishes an event that an adapter turns into the same request).
2. The Notification API authenticates the producer, checks its quota, and records the idempotency key. A repeat key returns the original `notification_id`.
3. The API appends the request to `requests-high` or `requests-normal` by its category's priority, and only then returns `202 Accepted`. Campaign pages arrive on a third topic from the fan-out service.
4. A separate processor pool consumes each request topic. The topics are partitioned by `user_id`, so all of one user's notifications in a lane go to the same processor instance.
5. The processor loads preferences, devices, and the template, applies the rules in [Respecting the human](#respecting-the-human), and either renders the notification now or hands it to the scheduler to release later.
6. It publishes one message per chosen channel to that channel's topic, in the lane matching its priority.
7. Channel workers send each message to its provider: APNs, FCM, the email provider, or the SMS provider.
8. For the in-app channel, the worker writes to the inbox store instead.
9. Providers report later outcomes (delivered, bounced, marked as spam) through webhooks; the tracking service records them in the status store.
</Steps>

Why this shape?

- **The API is thin.** It does four cheap things: authenticate, check the producer's quota, deduplicate, append. That is how we get 99.99% availability for ingestion even when a provider, the preference service, or a template is broken.
- **The request topics are shock absorbers.** A campaign launch or a provider outage turns into queue lag, not dropped requests. We use a log-based broker (Kafka or similar) because we want replay and per-partition ordering by user; message queues get their own chapter in Building blocks.
- **Priority is split at the door.** A login code and a campaign page never share a topic or a processor pool, so neither can delay the other. The channel lanes in [Fan-out and priority](#fan-out-and-priority) continue the same split down to the providers.
- **One topic per channel** isolates channels from each other. An email provider outage backs up the email topic; push keeps flowing.
- **The processor is where policy lives.** Preferences, caps, quiet hours, and channel choice are decided once, in one place, instead of in every producer.

## Deep dives

### Never lose a notification, rarely send one twice [#reliable-delivery]

Our durability promise starts before our API and ends after the provider. Let us walk the chain and find every place a notification can be lost or duplicated.

**Producer to API.** Consider the order service. It commits "order shipped" to its database, then calls our API. If it crashes between the two, the notification is lost. If it calls first and the commit fails, we notify about something that never happened. This is the dual-write problem, and the standard fix is the <Term id="transactional-outbox">transactional outbox</Term>: the producer writes the notification request into an outbox table in the same transaction as the business change, and a relay publishes it afterwards <Cite href="https://microservices.io/patterns/data/transactional-outbox" title="Pattern: Transactional outbox" author="Chris Richardson" />. The relay may publish the same row twice after a crash, which is fine, because the request carries an idempotency key.

**API to topic.** The API returns `202` only after the request topic has acknowledged the write on multiple replicas (for Kafka, `acks=all` with a minimum in-sync replica count of 2). If the broker is unreachable, the API returns `503` and the producer retries with the same idempotency key. We never acknowledge a request we have not durably stored.

The idempotency store maps `(producer_id, idempotency_key)` to a `notification_id` for 24 hours. The order of operations matters more than it looks. If the API appended to the topic first and recorded the key second, two concurrent retries could both append, with two different ids that nothing downstream could relate. If it recorded the key first and the append then failed, the key would point at a notification that never existed, and every later retry would get a confident `202` for a ghost. So the API does both, in this order:

1. Conditionally insert the key with a freshly generated `notification_id` and the state `pending`. If the key already exists, reuse its id.
2. Append the request, carrying that id, to the topic.
3. Mark the key `accepted`, best effort.

A retry that finds the key still `pending` repeats step 2 with the *same* id. The topic may then hold the request twice, which is at-least-once again, but both copies share one `notification_id`, and the processor's first stage drops the second. Every deterministic replay inside our boundary carries an id we can recognize; an unknown provider outcome remains the exception described below.

**Topic to processor to channel topics.** The processor publishes its outputs downstream first, and only then records the request's id as processed and commits its offset. A processor that crashes in between replays the request and republishes the same outputs, carrying the same ids. That is at-least-once again, and the next layer — the worker's send log — recognizes them.

<FigRef id="notification-lifecycle" /> shows the states one delivery moves through. Every path ends somewhere visible: `sent`, `delivered`, `suppressed`, `expired`, `dead_lettered`, or `indeterminate`. The last one deserves its name.

**Worker to provider: the unknown outcome.** This is the genuinely hard step. A worker sends a push to APNs and the request times out. Did Apple receive it? We cannot know. If we retry, the user might get it twice. If we do not, they might not get it at all. No protocol trick removes this choice; the best we can do is make it explicitly, record it, and make its cost small.

The send log is where we make it. Before calling a provider, the worker claims the row for `(notification_id, target)` with a conditional write. The rules are:

| Send-log row                     | What the worker does                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| absent                           | Create it as `attempting` with a fresh `attempt_id` and a lease (call timeout plus a margin); send.  |
| `attempting`, lease active       | Another worker is mid-call. Do not send; requeue with a delay.                                       |
| `attempting`, lease expired      | The previous worker crashed mid-call, so its outcome is unknown. Treat as `indeterminate`.           |
| `indeterminate`                  | Apply the category's policy: `retry` takes a new lease and sends again; `do_not_retry` stops here.   |
| `sent` or `failed`               | Already finished. Skip.                                                                              |

The lease is what separates "someone is calling Apple right now" from "someone was calling Apple and died". Without it, a redelivered message cannot tell the two apart, and must either wait forever or risk a concurrent duplicate. The `attempt_id` makes the final write safe: a worker marks the row `sent` only if the row still carries *its* attempt id, so a slow worker that wakes up after its lease expired cannot overwrite a newer attempt's outcome.

It marks the row indeterminate and requeues the message for a second attempt with a delay. When redelivered, the claim moves from indeterminate to attempting because the category allows a retry; the worker posts again with the same collapse id, receives 200 OK, and marks the row sent. <FigRef id="retry-and-dedup" /> walks one timed-out push through that table. Three more layers reduce the cost of the duplicates the send log cannot see — the timed-out request that actually succeeded:

1. **A provider-side collapse id.** APNs accepts an `apns-collapse-id` header and uses it to merge multiple notifications into a single notification for the user <Cite href="https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/sending-notification-requests-to-apns" title="Sending notification requests to APNs" author="Apple" />. On Android, the notification `tag` replaces an earlier entry in the drawer. We set both to the `notification_id`. This is mitigation, not a guarantee: it changes what the user sees in the notification list, not whether the phone already buzzed for the first copy. (FCM also has a `collapse_key`, which looks like the same thing and is not; see the provider table in [Living with providers](#living-with-providers).)
2. **The client.** When the app handles a push itself, it keeps the last few hundred `notification_id`s it has seen and ignores a repeat. This catches duplicates in data messages and in-app updates that the operating system would otherwise show twice.
3. **A per-category policy for channels without a collapse mechanism.** Email and SMS have no equivalent: a duplicate email is a duplicate email. So each category declares what an `indeterminate` outcome means. A login code says `retry`; a second copy of a code is harmless, a missing one locks the user out. A marketing email says `do_not_retry`; one lost promotion is better than a duplicate that gets reported as spam. The status API reports `indeterminate` honestly, and the indeterminate rate per provider is a metric.

Failing over to a second provider interacts with the same problem. After a *definitive* failure — connection refused, a `5xx` returned before the provider accepted anything — failover is safe. After a timeout, sending the same email through a second provider is a retry with a different name, and follows the same category policy.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Exactly-once is a trap here">
Exactly-once processing inside a broker does not extend to an HTTP call to Apple. Once a side effect leaves your system, the best you can do is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent or collapsible effects, and an honest record of what you do not know.
</Callout>

**Retries without blocking.** A failed send must wait before retrying, but a worker that sleeps holds its partition hostage: every message behind it waits too. Instead, the worker publishes the message to a **delay topic** and moves on. Each delay topic is consumed by workers that wait until the message's due time. The normal lane uses `retry-2s`, `retry-30s`, `retry-5m`, and so on; the high lane uses a single short delay of a few hundred milliseconds, because its latency budget (below) has room for exactly one retry. Delays follow <Term id="exponential-backoff">exponential backoff</Term> with random <Term id="jitter">jitter</Term>, so a provider recovering from an outage is not hit by a synchronized wall of retries <Cite href="https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/exponential-backoff-and-jitter/" title="Exponential Backoff And Jitter" author="Marc Brooker" year="2015" />.

What we retry depends on what the provider told us:

| Provider response                         | Meaning                       | Action                                         |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Timeout, connection reset after sending   | Unknown outcome               | Mark `indeterminate`; per-category policy      |
| `429` too many requests                   | Throttled                     | Retry with backoff; tell the rate limiter      |
| `5xx`                                     | Provider-side failure         | Retry with backoff                             |
| `400` bad request, payload too large      | Our bug                       | Do not retry; dead-letter and alert            |
| APNs `410`, FCM `UNREGISTERED`            | The token is dead             | Do not retry; delete the token                 |

After a fixed number of attempts, or once the notification's TTL passes, the message goes to a <Term id="dead-letter-queue">dead-letter queue</Term>. The DLQ is not a graveyard: it has an owner, an alert on its growth rate, and a replay tool, because the most common cause of dead letters is a bug we fix and then want to re-drive.

**What about ordering?** Users notice when "Your order was delivered" arrives before "Your order shipped". It is tempting to demand global ordering. We do not, for two reasons. First, it is expensive: a single ordered stream cannot be parallelized, and our throughput depends on hundreds of partitions processed independently. Second, it is not achievable end to end anyway, because APNs, FCM, and email providers make no ordering promise across separate requests, and a retry naturally reorders a message behind later ones.

What users actually need is narrower: **the latest state wins**. We get that with three cheap tools:
- Partitioning each request topic by `user_id` keeps one user's notifications in order through the processor, which is where aggregation needs it.
- A shared `collapse_key` ("order-8812") makes the newer notification replace the older one on the device, so a late "shipped" cannot sit on top of "delivered".
- Each message carries its creation time, and workers drop a message whose collapse key already has a newer one in the send log.

For a live, ordered stream inside an open app, the right tool is a sequence number per connection, which is how Uber's RAMEN delivers in-app messages <Cite href="https://www.uber.com/blog/real-time-push-platform/" title="Uber's Real-Time Push Platform" author="Uber Engineering" year="2020" />. That is a different system from this one.

### Fan-out and priority without head-of-line blocking [#fan-out-and-priority]

At 9:00 on a Monday, marketing launches a campaign to 50M users. At 9:01, a user tries to log in and waits for their one-time password (OTP). If both share a queue, the code sits behind millions of promotions. That is <Term id="head-of-line-blocking">head-of-line blocking</Term>, and it is the most common way notification platforms fail their most important messages.

**Lanes, from the front door to the provider.** Priority begins at ingestion: as <FigRef id="high-level-design" /> shows, the API routes each request to `requests-high` or `requests-normal`, campaign pages arrive on their own topic, and each stream has its own processor pool sized for its latency target. It is easy to draw the lanes only after the processor and stop, which leaves a hole: campaign expansion and login codes would share a processor pool, and a 50M-user expansion would delay every code *before* either reached a lane. After the processor, every channel topic is split again into `high`, `normal`, and `bulk` lanes, each with its own consumer pool, shown in <FigRef id="priority-lanes" />.

<Figure
  id="priority-lanes"
  src="./figures/priority-lanes.svg"
  alt="The notification processor writes to three priority lanes: high for OTP and security, normal for orders and social, and bulk for campaigns. Each lane has its own worker pool. All three pools pass through a shared provider rate limiter before reaching the provider; high and normal pools have a reserved share, the bulk pool uses leftover capacity only."
  caption="Channel lanes. Separate queues and worker pools keep bulk traffic from delaying urgent messages; a shared limiter divides the provider's quota between them."
  width="wide"
/>

Separate topics and pools give isolation in time: a backlog in `bulk` cannot delay a message in `high`. But all three lanes still share one provider quota, so the provider rate limiter divides it. `high` and `normal` get reserved shares; `bulk` gets whatever is left. When a burst of login codes arrives, the campaign slows down automatically. Netflix's RENO system uses the same idea, routing events into separate queues by priority with their own processing clusters <Cite href="https://www.infoq.com/news/2022/03/netflix-reno/" title="Netflix's RENO Keeps Experience Consistent across Devices" author="InfoQ" year="2022" />.

The rate limiter is a <Term id="token-bucket">token bucket</Term> per provider and lane (rate-limiting algorithms get their own chapter in Building blocks). With hundreds of workers, a central counter becomes a hot spot, so each worker leases a slice of the budget — "you may send 200 messages per second for the next 10 seconds" — and renews it. The limiter trades a little precision for not putting a network hop in front of every send. Leases also make the limiter adaptive. When a provider answers `429`, the coordinator halves the lane's total rate; after a quiet interval it adds back a small fixed step. That is <Term id="aimd">additive increase, multiplicative decrease (AIMD)</Term>, the same rule the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) uses for congestion: back off fast, probe back slowly, and settle a little under the provider's real limit, which is often lower than its published one.

**Where the five seconds go.** The high lane's target is a p99 of 5 s from acceptance to provider handoff. The work itself takes well under a second; the budget exists to absorb queueing and exactly one retry.

| Stage                                              | Typical      | Budget at p99 |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ------------- |
| API: authenticate, idempotency check, append       | 10–30 ms     | 100 ms        |
| Wait in `requests-high`                            | ≈ 0          | 500 ms        |
| Processor: cached lookups, rules, render           | 20–50 ms     | 300 ms        |
| Wait in the channel's `high` lane                  | ≈ 0          | 500 ms        |
| Rate limiter (reserved share)                      | ≈ 0          | 200 ms        |
| Attempt 1 (call timeout)                           | 100 ms       | 800 ms        |
| Backoff before the retry                           | —            | 400 ms        |
| Attempt 2 (call timeout)                           | —            | 800 ms        |
| Slack: pauses, rebalances                          | —            | 1.4 s         |
| **Total**                                          | **≈ 200 ms** | **5.0 s**     |

The high lane's call timeout is 800 ms, far shorter than the normal lane's, because a login code that takes longer than that is better retried than waited for. A notification that needs a third attempt still gets one, but it has left the objective and counts as a miss. The lag alerts for the two queue waits fire at their budgets, long before the user-visible target is missed.

**Campaign fan-out.** A campaign is one request that <Term id="fan-out">fans out</Term> into 50M notifications. We must not expand it into 50M messages in one transaction, and we must survive a crash halfway through without sending anyone two copies. <FigRef id="campaign-fanout" /> shows the flow.

<Figure
  id="campaign-fanout"
  src="./figures/campaign-fanout.svg"
  alt="The campaign service schedules a send at 09:00 local time. The scheduler starts one jittered fan-out job per IANA time-zone cohort after resolving its current UTC offset. Fan-out workers page through the segment store 1,000 user IDs at a time, checkpoint their cursor in a campaign progress store, and publish one message per page to the campaign-pages topic, keyed by a hash of the campaign and page. The notification processor expands each page into per-user notifications with an id derived from a hash of the campaign and user, keyed by user, and writes them to the bulk lane."
  caption="Campaign fan-out. Deterministic notification ids make every step safe to repeat; the page key spreads the work across partitions."
/>

<Steps>
1. The campaign is scheduled for 09:00 in each recipient's local time.
2. The scheduler groups recipients by IANA time zone, such as `America/Chicago`, resolves each zone's current UTC offset shortly before execution, and starts its fan-out job as local 09:00 arrives, with a few minutes of jitter. A global campaign becomes hundreds of small jobs spread across a day.
3. Each fan-out worker reads the segment in pages of 1,000 user IDs. 50M users is 50,000 pages.
4. After each page, it checkpoints its cursor. A crashed worker resumes from the last checkpoint, not from the beginning.
5. It publishes one message per page to the `campaign-pages` topic — 50,000 messages, not 50M — keyed by `hash(campaign_id, page_number)`. Keying by `campaign_id` alone would put every page of a campaign on one partition and make the whole fan-out as fast as one consumer.
6. The processor expands each page into individual notifications. The key move: each `notification_id` is `hash(campaign_id, user_id)`.
7. The resulting notifications are keyed by `user_id` from here on, so per-user ordering and aggregation work as for any other notification, and they enter the bulk lane.
</Steps>

Because the id is derived rather than random, every repeat of every step produces the *same* notification ids. A page replayed after a crash yields notifications whose ids already exist in the send log, so they are skipped. Deterministic ids turn "exactly once" from a coordination problem into a lookup.

At our 40k msg/s bulk budget, a 50M campaign takes about 21 minutes of send capacity. Time-zone bucketing helps here too: an audience spread across many offsets never needs all 50M sends in the same hour.

<Aside>
Segments are snapshotted when a fan-out job starts. A user who joins the segment ten minutes later is not included, and one who leaves is still sent to — unless they opted out, which the processor and the worker both check at send time regardless.
</Aside>

**Canary the campaign.** Before the fan-out runs at full speed, the scheduler releases the first page or two, a few thousand users, and pauses for a few minutes while the tracking service watches provider error rates, bounce and complaint rates, and the suppression rate. A broken deep link, a template with an unrendered `{{first_name}}`, or an audience that turns out to include opted-out users shows up in that sample at a cost of thousands of bad sends instead of millions. Marketing teams call this a seed or test send. We make it a mandatory stage of every bulk job rather than a courtesy.

**Scheduling: the timer problem.** Four features release something at a future time: quiet hours, "send at 09:00 local", digest windows, and escalation checks (all described below). Delay topics suit the short, fixed waits that retries need, but "07:00 tomorrow" can be ten hours away, and holding a message in a broker partition for ten hours blocks everything behind it. Long waits need a store, not a queue. <FigRef id="scheduler" /> shows the design.

<Figure
  id="scheduler"
  src="./figures/scheduler.svg"
  alt="The notification processor writes rows with a jittered due time, and the campaign service writes one row per IANA time-zone cohort after resolving its current offset, into a due-time table keyed by due minute and shard. Sweepers, each holding a lease on one minute and shard, read their shard and claim rows with a lease, publish each row to its target topic, and then mark it released. Rows whose TTL has already passed go to the suppression log as expired."
  caption="The scheduler. Sharding each minute spreads a busy minute across sweepers; the claim lease means a crash causes a repeat, never a loss."
/>

<Steps>
1. The processor (for quiet hours, send-at, digests, and escalations) or the campaign service (one row per IANA time-zone cohort) resolves the local civil time near execution and writes a row keyed by `((due_minute, shard), id)`, where `shard` is a hash of the id modulo 32. The row names its target topic: a channel lane for a rendered notification, a request topic for a digest to assemble, or the fan-out job queue for a campaign cohort.
2. Each sweeper holds a lease on one `(minute, shard)` pair, reads it, oldest minutes first, and claims each row with a conditional update from `pending` to `publishing`, with a lease of its own.
3. It publishes the row to its target topic.
4. Only then does it mark the row `released`.
</Steps>

The order of steps 2 to 4 is the whole point. Marking a row released *before* publishing would lose it forever if the sweeper crashed in between. Publishing first and marking second means a crash leaves the row in `publishing` with an expired lease; the next sweeper publishes it again, and the duplicate is recognized downstream by its id like every other duplicate in the system.

**How busy is a busy minute?** Assume 10% of notifications are held for later, about 100M a day, and that the largest group of users sharing one local release instant is 20% of the audience. Most users keep the default end of quiet hours, 07:00, so about 20M rows fall due at once. Without jitter, that is 20M rows in one minute — one hot partition, and a burst of 333k notifications per second into the lanes, nearly six times our peak. Spread over a 30-minute release window, it is about 670k rows per minute, or 11k per second; across 32 shards, about 21k rows per shard per minute, which one sweeper reads comfortably. **Jitter is not a nicety here, it is load management**, and the same applies to campaigns that every marketer schedules for 09:00 sharp: each time-zone cohort's job starts at a random point in a 15-minute window.

Two details matter in practice. We store an IANA zone, not a fixed UTC offset, and resolve the local target near execution so daylight-saving and government rule changes are included. A transition can make "local 07:00" happen twice or not at all, so the resolver picks the earlier occurrence when it repeats and the first valid instant after it when it does not exist. If the user changes zones before resolution, the new zone wins; after a row is released, we do not move it. And when the sweeper falls behind, it processes due minutes oldest first, and a notification whose TTL has passed by the time it is released goes to `expired` rather than to a user who no longer needs it.

### Respecting the human [#respecting-the-human]

Every notification interrupts someone. Send too many and users disable notifications for your app entirely, or mark your email as spam, which hurts delivery for everyone. The processor's most important job is deciding when not to send. <FigRef id="processor-pipeline" /> shows its pipeline.

<Figure
  id="processor-pipeline"
  src="./figures/processor-pipeline.svg"
  alt="Inside the notification processor, a request passes through seven stages in order: deduplicate, preferences and opt-outs, aggregate, select channels, frequency caps, render, and send now or hold. Duplicates, opted-out requests, and over-cap requests go to a suppression log. Aggregated notifications wait for their digest window in the scheduler; notifications in quiet hours or with a send-at time are also handed to the scheduler; the rest go to the channel topics."
  caption="The processor pipeline. Cheap checks that can drop a notification run first; caps run once the channel is known; rendering runs last."
/>

1. **Deduplicate.** Drop a notification whose `notification_id` has already been processed (the duplicates our own retries produce), or whose content, meaning the same user, template, and parameters, matches one accepted in the last few minutes (the duplicates a producer bug produces).
2. **Preferences and opt-outs.** Look up the user's matrix of category × channel. "Order updates: push yes, email yes, SMS no." Some categories, such as security alerts, cannot be disabled; preferences can only narrow their channels.
3. **Aggregate.** Ten "someone liked your photo" events in five minutes should become one notification: "Priya and 9 others liked your photo." The processor adds aggregatable notifications to a window keyed by `(user, aggregation_key)` in a shared store, and writes a scheduler row for the window's close. When the row fires, the digest is assembled from the stored window and re-enters the pipeline at stage 4, with the deterministic id `hash(user, aggregation_key, window_start)` so a replayed window cannot produce two digests.
4. **Select channels.** Among the channels the user allows, pick the cheapest one that will work: in-app if the app is open, push if the user has a valid device token, email otherwise, and SMS only for categories that require it. "The app is open" comes from the connection gateway's presence signal; if presence is unavailable, we assume the app is closed and use push, because a redundant push is cheaper than a missed one. Push goes to every active device, so a user with a phone and a tablet sees both; reading on one sends a silent push carrying the `notification_id` that clears the other.
5. **Frequency caps.** Now that the channel is known, apply per-user caps per category and channel, such as "at most 3 marketing pushes per day". A digest counts once, not once per event it summarizes.
6. **Render.** Fill the pinned, localized template with the user's data, enforce size limits (an APNs payload is limited to 4 KB), and escape all user data in HTML email.
7. **Send now or hold.** Quiet hours apply per channel: push and SMS make a sound, so at 02:00 for the user they are handed to the scheduler and released when quiet hours end — or dropped if their TTL will expire first. Email and in-app are silent and go now. High-priority categories bypass quiet hours. "Send at 09:00 local" works the same way.

Frequency caps are the stage most often built wrong, in three ways: counting before the channel is chosen, counting by a calendar day nobody lives in, and counting the same notification twice when the processor replays after a crash. A version that avoids all three:

```ts title="frequency-cap.ts"
// The set contains only notifications that consumed a slot, so it never
// grows beyond the cap. atomic() executes the callback as one transaction.
export async function withinCap(n: Decided, limit: number): Promise<boolean> {
  const day = localDate(n.decidedAt, n.user.timeZone);
  const key = `cap:${n.userId}:${n.category}:${n.channel}:${day}`;
  return kv.atomic(key, async (tx) => {
    if (await tx.sismember(key, n.notificationId)) return true;
    if ((await tx.scard(key)) >= limit) return false;
    await tx.sadd(key, n.notificationId);
    await tx.expire(key, 2 * 86_400, "NX");
    return true;
  });
}

function localDate(at: Date, timeZone: string): string {
  // "en-CA" formats as YYYY-MM-DD.
  return new Intl.DateTimeFormat("en-CA", { timeZone }).format(at);
}
```

Four details carry the correctness. A set of ids instead of a counter makes an allowed decision idempotent: a replay finds the same id and does not use another slot. Once the set reaches the cap, new ids are rejected without being stored, so an abusive producer cannot grow one hot key without bound. Running the membership check, size check, add, and expiry in one transaction prevents races and means a crash cannot leave a key that never expires. And the day is the user's civil day in their own time zone, because "3 per day" means something to a person in Chicago only if the day ends at their midnight, not at midnight in Greenwich. A notification counts against the cap when it is *decided*, not when it is delivered; one that later fails at the provider has still used a slot, which errs on the side of sending less.

The state is bounded by the rules, not by traffic. If 100M users each hit a cap of 3 in one day, the store holds at most 300M ids. At roughly 64 bytes per member including index overhead, that is about 19 GB before replication: large enough to shard and monitor, but independent of rejected volume. Categories without a cap do not create these sets.

The cap and the aggregation window live in a shared store, not in the processor's memory. Our priority lanes split a user's traffic across processor pools, and a rebalance or a crash moves partitions between instances; anything held only in memory would vanish with them. In the shared store, a crash delays an open digest until the scheduler row fires; it never drops it. LinkedIn's Air Traffic Controller takes the other route to the same property: state lives locally in each stream-processing task, backed by a changelog so a task can rebuild it on a new host <Cite href="https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/messaging-notifications/air-traffic-controller-member-first-notifications-at-linkedin" title="Air Traffic Controller: Member-first notifications at LinkedIn" author="Changji Shi, Adriel Fuad" year="2018" />. Local state is faster; the shared store is simpler to operate and is where we would start.

Every suppression is recorded with its reason. When a producer asks "why didn't my user get this?", the answer should be one query away: "suppressed: frequency cap, marketing push, 3 of 3 used today."

**The last check happens at the worker.** The processor decides at one moment, but a notification can sit for hours before it is sent: held for quiet hours, waiting in a campaign's bulk lane, or stuck behind a provider outage. A user who opts out in that window must not receive it. So STOP replies and unsubscribe clicks write to preferences synchronously and also add the user, category, and channel to a small **send-time suppression list**, replicated to every worker's cache. Each channel worker checks it immediately before calling the provider. It is one cache lookup per send, and it is what makes requirement 3 true: an opt-out beats every queue.

**Escalation: push first, SMS if unread.** For a few urgent categories — a fraud alert, "your driver is here" — the right channel is not one channel but a sequence. Push is free but unconfirmed: as [Living with providers](#living-with-providers) explains, APNs cannot tell us whether a phone in a tunnel received anything. SMS reaches that phone, at a cost. So the category declares an escalation: send push now, and write a scheduler row due two minutes later. When it fires, the processor checks for an "opened" receipt from the app's software development kit (SDK). If there is one, nothing happens. If not, it emits an SMS with the id `hash(notification_id, "escalate-sms")`, so a replayed check cannot send two. This ties three parts of the design together: the cost table explains why SMS is the fallback and not the default, the receipts explain what "unread" can mean, and the scheduler provides the timer. Without SDK receipts, escalation degrades into "always send SMS too", which costs what the cost table says it costs.

**When the preference service is down.** The processor cannot check opt-outs. Should it send anyway? The answer depends on the category, and the asymmetry is the point:

- **Marketing fails closed.** Sending a promotion to someone who opted out damages trust, can breach consent rules, and risks spam complaints. Hold bulk traffic until preferences are back; nobody misses a delayed promotion.
- **Transactional fails open, using the last cached preferences.** A login code that never arrives locks a user out. A stale cache entry is far better than no code.

<Callout type="tip" title="Write the failure policy into the category">
Make "fail open" or "fail closed", the meaning of an indeterminate outcome, quiet-hour bypass, escalation, sensitivity, and default TTL properties of each notification category, reviewed when the category is created. Then policy is data, not scattered `if` statements.
</Callout>

### Living with providers [#living-with-providers]

APNs, FCM, the email provider, and the SMS provider are the least reliable, least controllable part of our system, and every message goes through one of them. We wrap each in a **provider adapter**, shown in <FigRef id="provider-adapter" />.

<Figure
  id="provider-adapter"
  src="./figures/provider-adapter.svg"
  alt="A channel worker sends through a provider adapter containing a cached authentication token, an adaptive rate limiter, and a circuit breaker. With the breaker closed, requests go to the primary provider with the cached token; with it open, email and SMS fail over to a secondary provider, and push pauses its consumer. Both providers send delivery webhooks to a tracking service, which records state in a status store. Separately, the worker deletes tokens from the device registry when a provider reports them invalid."
  caption="A provider adapter: shared credentials, an adaptive rate limit, a circuit breaker, failover where it exists, and a feedback loop that keeps the device registry clean."

**Log in once, not once per send.** Both push providers authenticate senders with short-lived credentials. For APNs, the provider signs a JSON Web Token (JWT); Apple asks providers to refresh it no more than once every 20 minutes and at least once every 60, and rejects any token older than an hour with `403 ExpiredProviderToken` <Cite href="https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/establishing-a-token-based-connection-to-apns" title="Establishing a token-based connection to APNs" author="Apple" />. FCM's HTTP v1 API uses short-lived OAuth 2.0 access tokens. At 63k provider calls per second across hundreds of workers, the naive approach — each worker refreshing when its token expires — produces a synchronized stampede on the token endpoint, a burst of rejected sends, and an incident that looks exactly like a provider outage. The adapter holds one cached token per provider and credential, refreshes it early (around 45 minutes for APNs) with a single-flight refresh so only one refresh is ever in progress, and keeps using the old token until the new one is in hand.

**Connections are capacity.** Little's law said we need about 6.3k requests in flight at peak. APNs uses HTTP/2, so those requests share long-lived connections as concurrent streams. Apple's guidance is specific: reuse a connection for hours or days; open multiple connections when sending many notifications; do not assume a fixed number of concurrent streams per connection, because it varies with server load; resolve the APNs host name before each new connection; and spread traffic over many connections rather than bursting on a few <Cite href="https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/sending-notification-requests-to-apns" title="Sending notification requests to APNs" author="Apple" />. So each worker keeps a pool of warm connections, sized from the streams the server actually advertises, and the pool is part of capacity planning, not an implementation detail.

**Rate limits are someone else's numbers.** The adapter's limiter is configured from the provider's published quotas, not from our wishes. For FCM that means the project-wide per-minute quota (and a per-device limit: on Android, a burst of up to 240 messages per minute and 5,000 per hour to one device) <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/throttling-and-quotas" title="FCM throttling and quotas" author="Firebase" />. For SMS it means per-sender throughput. When a provider returns `429`, the adapter tightens its own budget rather than retrying harder.

**Speak each provider's dialect.** Our request fields map onto provider features that look alike and behave differently:

| Our field              | APNs (iOS)                                                     | FCM (Android)                                            |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| `priority`             | `apns-priority` header: 10 (immediate) or 5 (power-conserving) | `android.priority`: `high` or `normal`                   |
| `ttl_seconds`          | `apns-expiration`: an absolute time, honored best effort       | `android.ttl`: a duration, up to the 4-week default      |
| Replace on the device  | `apns-collapse-id`                                             | `android.notification.tag` replaces the drawer entry     |
| Replace while offline  | the same `apns-collapse-id`                                    | `android.collapse_key`; a device keeps at most 4 keys    |
| Payload limit          | 4 KB                                                           | 4 KB                                                     |

The Android column hides a trap for the dedup scheme in [reliable delivery](#reliable-delivery). FCM stores at most four distinct `collapse_key` values per device while it is offline, so a unique key per notification would make an offline phone silently keep only the four newest and drop the rest <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/customize-messages/collapsible-message-types" title="Non-collapsible and collapsible messages" author="Firebase" />. We therefore set `collapse_key` only to the producer's own `collapse_key`, where "shipped" and "delivered" should fold into one, and rely on the notification `tag` for duplicate suppression in the drawer.

The TTL row has two subtleties. FCM's default lifespan is four weeks <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/concept-options" title="About FCM messages" author="Firebase" />, so a worker that forgets to set `android.ttl` turns a ten-minute "driver arriving" alert into something that can land a month later. And Apple describes its expiration as best effort: a notification may be delivered after the time we set <Cite href="https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/sending-notification-requests-to-apns" title="Sending notification requests to APNs" author="Apple" />. So the payload also carries an `expires_at`, and the app discards a notification that arrives after it.

**Circuit breakers, bulkheads, and pausing.** Recall the concurrency estimate: a provider slowing from 100 ms to 1 s needs ten times the in-flight requests. Without protection, workers pile up waiting on the sick provider. Each adapter therefore has its own connection pool (a bulkhead) and a <Term id="circuit-breaker">circuit breaker</Term>: after a threshold of failures or timeouts it opens, fails new calls immediately for a cool-down period, then lets a few trial requests through <Cite href="https://martinfowler.com/bliki/CircuitBreaker.html" title="CircuitBreaker" author="Martin Fowler" />.

What a worker does *while* the breaker is open matters as much as opening it. Failing each message fast and sending it to the retry topic sounds reasonable and is a disaster at our rates: 52k push sends a second would cycle through the retry topics, use up their attempts in seconds, and fill the DLQ with perfectly good notifications. The breaker is telling us something about the *provider*, not about any one message. So for a provider with no alternative — APNs, FCM — the worker **pauses consumption** of that channel's partitions while the breaker is open. Messages wait in the topic, where they are durable and cheap, and their TTLs keep running. When a trial request succeeds and the breaker closes, the worker resumes from where it stopped. Retry topics are for one message's bad luck; pausing is for a provider's bad day.

**Failover where it exists.** Email and SMS are commodities: we can contract two providers and fail over when the breaker opens, subject to the unknown-outcome rule above. Push is not. There is exactly one way to reach an iPhone's notification center, and it is APNs. When APNs is down, we queue (within each notification's TTL) and deliver the important updates through the in-app inbox when users open the app. Netflix's RENO combines best-effort pushes to online devices with periodic pulls by the app for this reason <Cite href="https://www.infoq.com/news/2022/03/netflix-reno/" title="Netflix's RENO Keeps Experience Consistent across Devices" author="InfoQ" year="2022" />.

**Token hygiene.** Device tokens change when apps are reinstalled or restored, and die when apps are deleted. APNs answers a request for a dead token with HTTP `410` and reason `Unregistered` <Cite href="https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/handling-notification-responses-from-apns" title="Handling notification responses from APNs" author="Apple" />; FCM's HTTP v1 API returns `UNREGISTERED`. The adapter looks the token up in `device_by_token` and deletes the device. Firebase also recommends treating tokens as stale when a device has not been active for a long period — over a month in their guidance — and pruning or refreshing them proactively, because sends to stale tokens waste quota and make delivery metrics look worse than they are <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/manage-tokens" title="Best practices for FCM registration token management" author="Firebase" />. Our `last_seen` column exists for exactly this.

**The badge is a number we assert.** The `badge` value in an APNs payload is "the number to display" on the app icon, and `0` removes it <Cite href="https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/generating-a-remote-notification" title="Generating a remote notification" author="Apple" />. It is not an increment. Whatever we send replaces what the user sees, so a drifted `unread_count` becomes a lie on the home screen. The worker includes a badge only when the cached count is fresh, recomputing it from `inbox_by_user` when it is older than a few minutes, and the app sets its own badge from the inbox whenever it opens. The server's number is a hint; the inbox is the truth.

**Webhooks are a distributed system too.** Provider callbacks are themselves delivered at least once and in no particular order: a "delivered" event can arrive before our own worker has recorded "sent", and the same "bounced" event can arrive three times. The tracking service therefore treats status as a one-way state machine (<FigRef id="notification-lifecycle" />). It applies an event only if it moves the delivery forward, so a late "sent" never overwrites "delivered", and a repeated event changes nothing. Webhook endpoints verify each provider's signature, acknowledge quickly, and push events onto a topic for processing, because a provider that sees slow responses from us will retry and make the backlog worse.

**What "delivered" means depends on the channel.** Every channel reports something different, and a status API that hides the difference lies to its callers:

| Channel     | What the provider confirms                                       | What it cannot tell you                                                        |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Push (APNs) | It accepted the request for a valid token                        | Whether the device received or showed it; there is no per-message receipt      |
| Push (FCM)  | It accepted the request; aggregate delivery statistics exist     | Per-message receipt on the device                                              |
| Email       | The receiving mail server accepted it; bounces and complaints later | Inbox or spam folder; opens rely on tracking pixels that mail clients increasingly block |
| SMS         | A carrier delivery receipt, where the destination network provides one | Whether anyone read it                                                   |
| In-app      | Exact: we wrote the row and we store `read_at`                   | Nothing; this is the only channel we fully observe                             |

So `delivered` in our status store means different things per channel, and the status API says which. For push, the only reliable sign that a notification reached a person is the app's own telemetry: the SDK reports "notification opened" with the `notification_id`, and the tracking service joins it to the delivery. That receipt exists only if the client team instruments it, which is a conversation to have before launch, not after. Escalation depends on it.

**Email deliverability is a reputation system.** Mailbox providers judge every sender. Gmail requires senders of marketing and subscribed messages to support one-click unsubscribe, and asks bulk senders to keep their user-reported spam rate below 0.30%, recommending below 0.10% <Cite href="https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126" title="Email sender guidelines" author="Google" />. One-click unsubscribe is standardized by the `List-Unsubscribe` and `List-Unsubscribe-Post: List-Unsubscribe=One-Click` headers <Cite href="https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8058" title="RFC 8058: Signaling One-Click Functionality for List Email Headers" author="J. Levine, T. Herkula" year="2017" />. Email providers watch the same signals: Amazon SES places an account under review when its bounce rate reaches 5% and may pause sending at 10% <Cite href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ses/latest/dg/reputationdashboardmessages.html" title="Reputation metrics messages" author="Amazon Web Services" />.

For our design, that means concrete mechanisms:
- Bounce and complaint webhooks flow into the tracking service, which immediately adds the address to the suppression list.
- The unsubscribe endpoint writes to preferences and to the send-time suppression list, so it beats every queue.
- Transactional and marketing email go out from separate sending domains or IP pools, so a bad campaign cannot hurt password resets.
- Every sending domain publishes the three standard authentication records: Sender Policy Framework (SPF), DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM), and Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (DMARC). Gmail requires all three of bulk senders, and unauthenticated mail is the fastest route to the spam folder <Cite href="https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126" title="Email sender guidelines" author="Google" />.
- Hard bounces (the address does not exist) suppress the address permanently. Soft bounces (mailbox full, server busy) retry with backoff like any other transient failure.

**SMS has its own rules.** A reply of STOP must stop messages; providers enforce this at their edge and send us an inbound webhook, which writes to preferences and the suppression list the same way the email unsubscribe does. Length is money: a segment holds 160 characters in the GSM 7-bit default alphabet (GSM-7) and 70 in UCS-2, the 16-bit encoding needed for emoji and most non-Latin scripts, so a single emoji can double the cost of a login code. SMS templates are linted for segment count before they can be published, and the cost table in [Back-of-the-envelope estimation](#back-of-the-envelope-estimation) is the reason.

**Sender capacity has lead times measured in weeks.** The FCM quota steps in [Back-of-the-envelope estimation](#back-of-the-envelope-estimation) are one example of a broader rule: much of our capacity cannot be bought on launch day.

| Capacity                                      | What it takes                                                                 |
| --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| FCM standing quota above 600k/min             | +25% per request, each after usage regularly reaches 80% of the current quota <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/throttling-and-quotas" title="FCM throttling and quotas" author="Firebase" /> |
| FCM temporary quota above 18M/min             | At least 30 days' notice; at most 2 events and 30 days a year                 |
| A new dedicated email IP                      | Amazon SES's standard warm-up is a fixed 45-day ramp, and a new sending domain needs warming too <Cite href="https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/messaging-and-targeting/guide-to-ip-and-domain-warming-and-migrating-to-amazon-ses/" title="Guide to IP and domain warming and migrating to Amazon SES" author="Tyler Holmes" year="2025" /> |
| A US short code                               | About 4–8 weeks of carrier approval after payment, plus internal review <Cite href="https://www.twilio.com/en-us/guidelines/us/short-code" title="United States: Short code guidelines" author="Twilio" /> |
| US 10-digit long codes (10DLC)                | Brand and campaign registration; unregistered traffic has been blocked since September 2023 <Cite href="https://www.twilio.com/en-us/changelog/-u-s--a2p-10dlc--full-blocking-of-traffic-sent-from-unregistered" title="US A2P 10DLC is now a fully verified route" author="Twilio" year="2023" /> |

So the ramp is part of the design. A new marketing domain sends a small, highly engaged audience first and grows over weeks; the launch plan for a new SMS use case starts with the registration, not the code. And because consent is part of registration — carriers ask what users agreed to — the consent record described in [Security and privacy](#security-and-privacy) is a prerequisite for capacity as well as a compliance requirement.

Deliverability problems are invisible to your own success metrics. The provider accepted the email, and it still went to spam. Track complaint and bounce rates per sending domain as first-class service-level indicators.
</Callout>

## Scaling and bottlenecks

Start from the peak of 58k notifications and 63k provider attempts per second, and ask what breaks first.

**At today's peak:**
- **Request topics.** Assume one processor instance handles about 1k notifications per second including lookups. That is about 60 instances at peak, split across the three pools by their traffic; we provision 128 partitions for `requests-normal` and 32 each for `requests-high` and `campaign-pages`, so each pool can double its consumers without repartitioning.
- **Broker throughput.** At about 1 KB per message, 58k msg/s is roughly 58 MB/s into the request topics and about the same again across the channel topics, well within a modest broker cluster. The broker is not the bottleneck; the services behind it and the providers beyond them are.
- **Lookups.** 58k preference reads and 58k device reads per second. Both are served from cache; at a 95% hit rate the databases see about 2.9k reads per second each.
- **Status writes.** The conservative ceiling is 232k writes per second, and "batch them" is not an answer: in a wide-column store, each row in a different partition is its own write, and grouping rows from different partitions into one request saves nothing. The deliveries table is partitioned by `notification_id`, which spreads writes evenly. Assume a node sustains about 15k small writes per second (replace this with your own benchmark): with three replicas that is 232k × 3 ÷ 15k ≈ 46 nodes, call it 50 before headroom. Workers and the tracking service publish status events to a topic, and a writer drains it into the store, so a slow status store causes status lag, never a lost or delayed send. We scale the real cluster from the measured events-per-notification ratio; 50 nodes is the upper-bound estimate, not a promise that every channel emits four events.
- **Hot users.** A viral post can generate millions of "liked your photo" events for one account in minutes, all landing on one partition. Aggregation collapses them into a handful of digests and the per-user cap bounds the rest, so the partition spends its time counting rather than sending. A cap on raw events per user per minute at the API protects the partition from the counting too.
- **FCM quota.** The 1.56M per-minute Android peak needs five standing increases from the default, each requested only after usage sits near the previous limit. Until then, the bulk lane absorbs the shortfall by slowing down.

**At ×10 (580k msg/s, 10B notifications a day):**
- Processors, channel workers, and topics scale horizontally; their per-user state already lives in shared stores, so adding instances is a rebalance, not a migration.
- Provider quotas dominate. Android peak becomes about 15.6M messages per minute, 26 times FCM's default quota, reachable only through a long series of standing increases planned with the provider. SMS needs many registered senders and usually several providers.
- The status store grows to hundreds of nodes. At that point, recording only the transitions a caller can see — accepted, sent, delivered, failed — and keeping intermediate states only in the send log becomes worth the reduced detail.
- Aggregation and caps become *capacity* features, as well as user-experience features: every notification we merge or suppress is one we do not pay to send.

**At ×100:**
- Android peak passes 150M messages per minute. Firebase's temporary event quota, for which it asks at least 30 days' notice above 18M per minute, is capped at two events a year <Cite href="https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging/throttling-and-quotas" title="FCM throttling and quotas" author="Firebase" />. It can cover a launch, not a normal Monday. Standing capacity at this scale is a relationship with the provider, planned like any other critical dependency.
- A single region becomes the bottleneck and the risk; the next section designs for that.
- Campaigns are no longer something the platform can push "as fast as possible". They become scheduled workloads with planned capacity, like batch jobs on a shared cluster.

### Regions [#regions]

Running in several regions protects against losing one, but only if we are precise about what each region owns and what happens to work in flight. <FigRef id="region-failover" /> shows the design.

<Figure
  id="region-failover"
  src="./figures/region-failover.svg"
  alt="A strongly consistent user directory records each user's home region and epoch and grants a renewable ownership lease. Region A's topics and state replicate asynchronously to Region B. After Region A can no longer renew its lease, the directory assigns epoch 8 and a new lease to Region B; producers route there, and only workers with a valid lease may send to providers."
  caption="Region failover. Epochs order ownership changes; a renewable authoritative lease makes an isolated old region stop sending."
/>

<Steps>
1. A small, strongly consistent **user directory** records each user's home region and epoch and grants the owning regional shard a short renewable lease. Region B cannot take ownership until region A's lease has expired; workers that cannot renew stop before making another provider call.
2. Producers route new requests for those users to region B's API.
3. Region B's processors resume from the mirrored request topics, and its workers claim sends with the new epoch only while their ownership lease remains valid.
</Steps>

**What each region owns.** Everything that makes a notification correct is per user, so it lives in the user's home region: idempotency keys, the send log, caps, scheduler rows, the inbox. A request for a user homed elsewhere is forwarded once, at the API, to the home region's request topic. Within a region, durability comes from replication across three availability zones, which is what makes the `202` honest. Across regions, the request topics are mirrored and the state stores replicated **asynchronously**, typically seconds behind.

**Fencing the old region.** The danger in failover is not only losing work; it is two regions sending the same work. An epoch alone is insufficient during a network partition: an isolated region A could keep accepting writes into its old local store without seeing epoch 8 in region B. The directory therefore grants a short ownership lease to each regional shard. The provider adapter checks the locally cached lease deadline before every external call and fails closed when it expires; renewing requires a quorum response from the directory. Failover waits for the old lease to expire, increments the epoch, and grants region B a new lease. Every send-log claim also carries that epoch, so a returning region cannot replay old claims. Clock uncertainty is included in the lease safety margin. This is the same idea as a <Term id="fencing-token">fencing token</Term>, with the lease closing the split-brain window before the irreversible provider call.

**What failover costs.** We state it in the two standard numbers. The <Term id="rto">recovery time objective (RTO)</Term> is minutes: wait out the ownership lease, flip the directory, warm the consumers, resume. The <Term id="rpo">recovery point objective (RPO)</Term> is the replication lag, a few seconds, and it has two concrete effects. A notification accepted in region A whose request had not yet been mirrored is lost — the one exception to our durability promise, and the reason it says "short of losing an entire region". A notification region A sent whose send-log row had not yet replicated may be sent again by region B, and its category's indeterminate-outcome policy decides whether that is acceptable. If a category cannot accept either, the high lane can replicate its request topic synchronously across regions, at the cost of a cross-region round trip on every login code. That is a trade we make per category, not for the platform.

**Consent lags too.** An opt-out written in region A a second before it failed may not have reached region B. A marketing send to that user is a message they refused, and the consent rules that matter most are about exactly that. So after a failover, region B holds marketing for the affected users until replication has caught up, or for a fixed grace period when the old region cannot be read at all. Security alerts keep the fail-open policy the categories already declare. It is the same asymmetry as the preference-service outage, applied to geography.

## Reliability and failure modes

| Component                     | Failure                                  | Detection                               | Mitigation                                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Push, email, or SMS provider  | Outage or 5xx                            | Error rate per adapter; breaker opens   | Pause consumption for push; fail over for email and SMS; TTLs keep running |
| Provider                      | Slow responses                           | Latency p99 per adapter                 | Timeouts, bulkheads, breaker; never let one provider consume all workers   |
| Provider authentication       | Token refresh stampede or expiry         | `403 ExpiredProviderToken`, auth errors | One cached token per credential; early single-flight refresh               |
| Request topic                 | Broker or partition loss                 | Under-replicated partitions; produce errors | Replication factor 3, `acks=all`; the API returns `503` and producers retry |
| Processor or worker           | Crash mid-batch                          | Consumer lag; restarts                  | Replay from last committed offset; send log skips duplicates               |
| Worker                        | Crash mid-call to a provider             | Expired leases in the send log          | Lease expiry marks the attempt indeterminate; category policy decides      |
| Any consumer                  | Poison message (bad template, bad data)  | Repeated failures on one message        | Bounded retries, then dead-letter; never block the partition               |
| Preference service            | Unavailable                              | Lookup errors                           | Marketing fails closed; transactional fails open with cached preferences   |
| User                          | Opts out after a send was decided        | Suppression hits at the worker          | Send-time suppression list checked before every provider call             |
| A producer                    | Runaway loop sends millions of requests  | Per-producer rate and anomaly alerts    | Per-producer quotas at the API; content dedup and per-user caps            |
| Device registry               | Dead tokens accumulate                   | Rising `410` and `UNREGISTERED` rates   | Delete via `device_by_token`; prune stale tokens by `last_seen`            |
| One user (hot partition)      | Millions of events for one account       | Lag skew across partitions              | Aggregation and caps; per-user event cap at the API                        |
| Scheduler                     | Sweeper crashes or falls behind          | Age of the oldest unreleased due minute | Publish before marking released; leases; oldest-first; past-TTL rows expire |
| Scheduler                     | Everyone due at 07:00 or 09:00           | Rows per due minute                     | Jittered release windows; 32 shards per minute                             |
| Tracking service              | Webhook flood during a provider incident | Webhook topic lag                       | Acknowledge fast, process from a topic; status is monotonic, so order does not matter |
| SMS                           | Pumping fraud inflates traffic and spend | SMS spend and volume by destination country | Country allow-list, velocity limits, spend alerts                      |
| Region                        | Region loss or network partition         | Health checks; ownership-lease expiry   | Workers fail closed without a lease; directory waits out the old lease, then grants a new epoch; RPO of seconds |

The runaway producer deserves a special mention because it is the incident almost every notification team has: a bug in someone else's service sends the same notification in a loop. The idempotency key does not help if the bug generates a new key each time. Content dedup in the processor, per-producer quotas at the API, per-user caps, and a large red "pause this category" switch for on-call engineers are what stop a bug from reaching millions of phones.

## Observability

We measure the system the way users experience it: per priority lane, per channel, per provider.

**Service-level objectives:**
- 99.99% of API requests succeed (excluding producer errors).
- High lane: p99 time from acceptance to provider handoff under 5 s.
- Normal lane: p99 under 60 s. Bulk lane: campaigns finish within their planned window.

**Key metrics and alerts:**
- **Consumer lag per topic and lane**, in seconds rather than messages. Lag in `high` pages someone; lag in `bulk` during a campaign is expected.
- **Provider outcomes by status code.** A jump in `429` means a quota problem; a jump in `410` means a token-hygiene problem; a jump in `400` means we shipped a bug; a jump in `403 ExpiredProviderToken` means our credential refresh broke.
- **Indeterminate rate per provider.** The share of attempts whose outcome we could not know. It rises before a provider declares an incident.
- **Send-log skip rate.** How often a worker found work already done. A sudden rise means something upstream is replaying.
- **Suppression rate by reason**, including send-time suppressions. A sudden rise in "frequency cap" suppressions usually means a producer changed behavior.
- **Scheduler health:** rows per due minute and the age of the oldest unreleased minute.
- **DLQ growth rate**, with an owner.
- **Email complaint and bounce rates** per sending domain, alerting well before provider thresholds.
- **SMS spend per day and by destination country**, because a bug in SMS routing is the most expensive bug in the system, and fraud shows up first as a new country in the spend chart.
- **The delivery funnel** per channel and category: accepted → queued or suppressed → sent → delivered → opened. The ratios are the health metrics. A falling sent-to-accepted ratio means a provider or quota problem; a falling opened-to-sent ratio means a relevance or content problem. Opens come from the app SDK for push and in-app, from carrier receipts for SMS, and from tracking pixels for email, which mail clients increasingly block, so email open rates are a trend to watch rather than a number to trust.

**Tracing.** The `notification_id` is the correlation id from the producer's request through every topic, worker, and provider call. We also log the provider's own message id (for APNs, the `apns-id` response header) so a single notification can be traced with the provider's support team. Ids are logged; content is not (see below).

## Security and privacy

- **Producer authentication and authorization.** Producers authenticate with service identities (mutual Transport Layer Security or signed tokens). Each producer may send only the categories it owns, so the marketing service cannot send "security alert" notifications that bypass opt-outs.
- **Secrets in notifications.** A login code is a secret, and our pipeline is built to remember things: notification records live 90 days, logs live longer, and dead letters are replayable. Categories marked `sensitive` change all three. Their parameters are encrypted, kept only for the notification's own TTL — minutes for a login code — and never logged. Status queries report that a code was sent, never which code. The record keeps the `notification_id`, which is safe to log because it is an identifier, not the content.
- **SMS pumping fraud.** Also called artificially inflated traffic (AIT): attackers abuse phone-number fields — sign-up forms, "text me a link" — to make a service send messages to numbers they control, and share in the revenue those messages generate <Cite href="https://www.twilio.com/docs/messaging/features/sms-pumping-protection-programmable-messaging" title="SMS Pumping Protection for Programmable Messaging" author="Twilio" />. With SMS at 94% of our provider spend, this is the costliest attack on the platform, and it looks like success: sends succeed, providers deliver, the bill grows. Defenses sit at three layers. Products that let anonymous users trigger an SMS put velocity limits on those flows per destination number, per number prefix, and per requesting IP address or device. Our API enforces an allow-list of destination countries the product actually serves, and a per-prefix rate limit. And SMS spend by destination country is alerted, because a pumping attack usually announces itself as traffic to a country you have never sent to. Where possible, offer push or an authenticator app instead of SMS for login codes; it is cheaper and harder to abuse.
- **Template injection.** User-supplied data is always escaped when rendered, especially in HTML email. Templates are reviewed like code.
- **The lock screen is public.** Push content can appear on a locked phone in a meeting room. Never put sensitive details — balances, medical information, message bodies from sensitive conversations — in the push text. Send "You have a new message" and let the app fetch details after the user unlocks it.
- **Data minimization and retention.** Template parameters often contain personal data. We keep them only as long as status queries need them, keep long-term analytics on ids and outcomes, and let row TTLs delete the rest.
- **Account deletion.** Deleting a user removes their devices, preferences, inbox, and pending scheduled notifications.
- **Consent records.** For marketing email and SMS, we store when and how each user opted in. Consent rules vary by country and channel, carriers ask about consent when registering SMS senders, and replication lag can make a recent opt-out invisible in another region (see [Regions](#regions)). The platform's job is to make the consent record easy to keep and impossible to bypass.
- **Provider credentials.** APNs signing keys, FCM service accounts, and email and SMS API keys live in a secrets manager and are rotated. Only the provider adapters can read them.

## Trade-offs and alternatives

<Tradeoffs>
| Decision                    | Chosen                                    | Alternative                         | Why                                                                                     |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| API contract                | `202` after durable append                | Synchronous send                    | Producers get fast, reliable acceptance; delivery problems become queue lag, not errors |
| Delivery semantics          | At-least-once + dedup at every layer      | Exactly-once                        | Exactly-once cannot extend past the provider boundary; dedup is cheaper and honest      |
| Unknown outcomes            | Explicit `indeterminate` state + category policy | Treat timeouts as failures   | A timeout is not a failure; pretending it is either loses or duplicates silently        |
| Send claims                 | Leases with attempt ids                   | A plain "sent" flag                 | Separates "in flight" from "crashed"; a slow worker cannot overwrite a newer attempt    |
| Queue                       | Log-based broker partitioned by user      | Per-message queue (SQS-style)       | Replay and per-user ordering for aggregation                                            |
| Priority                    | Lanes from ingestion to provider          | One queue with a priority field     | Isolation: a backlog in bulk cannot block the high lane at any stage                    |
| Retries                     | Delay topics with backoff and jitter      | Sleep and retry in the worker       | Sleeping blocks every message behind it in the partition                                |
| Provider down, no alternative | Pause consumption                       | Fail and retry each message         | Retrying a dead provider burns attempts and floods the DLQ                              |
| Campaign fan-out            | Paged batches + deterministic ids         | Expand all recipients up front      | Crash-safe and resumable; replays are free                                              |
| Long delays                 | Sharded due-time table with jitter        | Delay topics for everything         | An hours-long hold in a partition blocks everything behind it                           |
| Frequency caps              | After channel selection, set of ids, user's local day | Counter before channel selection | Correct channel, no double counting on replay, days that match the user's       |
| Aggregation state           | Shared store + scheduler row              | Processor memory                    | Survives rebalances and crashes; local changelogged state is the faster alternative     |
| Notification storage        | Wide-column store, TTL, partitioned by user | Relational with indexes           | Write volume and automatic expiry at 1B/day; relational is right for v1                 |
| Inbox                       | Its own `inbox_by_user` table             | Filter the notifications table      | A wide-column store cannot filter a partition by channel; pages are range reads         |
| Preference outage           | Fail closed for marketing, open for transactional | One policy for all          | The cost of a wrong send and of a missed send differ by category                        |
| Provider redundancy         | Secondary providers for email and SMS     | Single provider per channel         | Commodity channels make failover cheap; push has no alternative                         |
| Android collapse            | `tag` for the drawer, producer key for `collapse_key` | Unique `collapse_key` per notification | FCM keeps only four collapse keys per offline device                      |
| Regions                     | Home region per user, async replication, renewable ownership lease + epoch | Synchronous cross-region writes | Lease prevents split-brain sends; seconds of RPO for most traffic; pay cross-region latency only where needed |
</Tradeoffs>

## Evolution

Nobody builds the final design first, and most teams should not build it at all — at first.

**Before v1 — buy.** Hosted notification platforms such as Braze, Customer.io, OneSignal, Courier, or Knock, or a cloud fan-out service such as Amazon SNS paired with an email and an SMS provider, cover templates, preferences, campaigns, and provider integrations for one product. Buy while one product team owns notifications. Build when the reasons in [High-level design](#high-level-design) start to bite: several products must share one frequency policy, one provider quota, and one consent record; notifications are core product behavior with latency targets a vendor will not commit to; or per-message vendor pricing at your volume exceeds the cost of a platform team. Buying gives up control of lanes and objectives, data residency, and pricing at scale. Building gives up a team, permanently.

**v1 — a small team, a few million notifications a day.**
- One notification service with the same public API.
- A relational `notifications` table doubles as the queue: workers claim rows with `SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`. The inbox is a filtered query on the same table.
- One provider per channel, preferences in the same database, retries with backoff, a single priority split (transactional versus everything else).
- This is boring and correct, and it is the right place to stay until shared policy and shared quota justify more. The API contract — `202`, idempotency keys, categories, TTLs — is the part that must be right from day one, because producers will depend on it for years.

**v2 — the platform.**
- Move to a log-based broker with request topics and channel topics split by priority.
- Add the send log with leases, delay topics, and a DLQ with a replay tool.
- Add the processor pipeline: preferences cache, aggregation, caps, quiet hours, and campaign fan-out with the scheduler.
- Add the tracking service and provider webhooks, so status means "delivered", not "we called someone".
- Start the sender registrations, domain warm-ups, and quota steps for the next year's volume.

**v3 — global scale.**
- Run in multiple regions with users homed by id, protected by renewable ownership leases and epoch fencing, and using consent-aware failover.
- Add secondary email and SMS providers with automatic failover.
- Add learned send-time optimization and relevance models that decide whether a notification is worth sending at all — the direction LinkedIn describes for its platform.
- Add a persistent connection channel for real-time in-app delivery. Uber's RAMEN platform shows the shape: at-least-once delivery over long-lived connections with sequence numbers and acknowledgments, at more than 1.5M concurrent connections <Cite href="https://www.uber.com/blog/real-time-push-platform/" title="Uber's Real-Time Push Platform" author="Uber Engineering" year="2020" />.

## Summary

| Requirement                         | How the design meets it                                                              |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1B/day; 58k notifications and 63k provider attempts per second at peak | Thin API, partitioned topics, horizontally scaled workers, capacity counted per device send |
| Never lose an accepted notification | `202` only after a replicated append; outbox for producers; publish-then-release in the scheduler; every path ends in a recorded state |
| Rarely duplicate                    | Idempotency keys, processor id check, leased send log, collapse ids, client dedup, per-category policy |
| Login codes in under 5 s at p99     | Priority from ingestion to provider, reserved provider share, a budget with room for one retry |
| 50M-user campaigns                  | Canary, paged fan-out spread across partitions, deterministic ids, jittered starts, bulk lane |
| Respect users                       | Preferences, caps per channel and local day, digests, quiet hours, send-time opt-out check |
| Survive providers                   | Real quotas, AIMD limits, cached credentials, breakers that pause, failover where it exists, token hygiene |
| Survive region loss                 | Home regions, async replication, renewable ownership leases, epoch fencing, an explicit RPO of seconds |
| Control cost and fraud              | Prefer push and in-app; SMS only where a category requires it; escalation instead of SMS by default; pumping defenses |

- Accept fast and durably; deliver asynchronously.
- Assume at-least-once everywhere, make duplicates detectable or harmless, and record what you cannot know.
- Isolate by priority from the front door to the provider, and by channel, so one backlog cannot block another.
- Put policy in one place — the processor — check consent again at the last moment, and make suppression explainable.
- Design around provider limits, lead times, and prices, because they are the real ceiling and the real bill.
- Start by buying, then with a relational table as a queue; earn the broker, the lanes, and the regions.
